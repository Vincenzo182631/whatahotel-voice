Reviewed by
Kathryn Romeyn
Why book?
Time tested and as ravishing as any new build, this archetypal Balinese resort—the very first luxury hotel in Ubud and second-ever Aman—masterfully blends vernacular architecture of the island with chic elegance and warm approachability. It's a heavenly, sacred jungle setting, where orchids reach out of tree trunks, fragrant frangipani blossoms seem to rain from the cerulean sky, and incensed Balinese Hindu offerings are as ubiquitous as cicada harmonies.
Set the scene
Along with the understated minimalism signature to Aman, Amandari feels like an extension of Kedewatan village, a traditional little gem north of Ubud center where, like at the resort, Balinese Hindu rituals take precedence. As much as this property is a true five-star hospitality experience, it also acts as an immersion into the aesthetics, flavors, scents, and sounds that are as core to the island as its beaches, monkeys, and rice fields. Traditionally thatched alang-alang roofs peek shaggy and friendly over paras stone walls like in a typical village, picking up lacy shadows of tree branches and palm fronds that shift constantly with the sun. Ancient looking statues are kissed by lichen and moss, temples are dressed in saffron, and the profuse amount of flora feels like it, too, has been here for ages.
Guests—the vast majority couples, but also families—stroll about looking at peace, unhurried, and in awe of their surroundings, simultaneously hushed yet alive with nature and the hum of bamboo xylophones called rindik.
Across nearly 25 acres and 180 feet above the Ayung River Gorge, Amandari is intimate enough that staff addressed me respectfully by name as Ibu Kathryn—"ibu" meaning Mrs. or mother. Yet it’s also big enough to be a significant place for the community. The name means “peaceful spirits,” and comes with a legend that hundreds of years ago, venerated Indian priest Maha Rsi Markandhya (who eventually spread Hinduism in Bali) had come to this place after a revelation received during meditation, bringing with him a white tiger from Java. The story is written on wooden placards and depicted in a stone carving near the lobby, where a replica tiger statue greets all arrivals, blanketed in a black-and-white checked cloth called kain poleng. Incense smoke curls up beside it from a flower-topped canang sari offering placed there several times a day by Ibu Lipat, who has been responsible for the ubiquitous Balinese Hindu ritual since the resort’s opening.
Even without the power of suggestion influencing me, I was seduced by the property’s soothing energy and sheer beauty. It claims the first infinity pool in Bali—a shapely one inspired by the iconic emerald rice paddies—something that back in the late ’80s required around 100 local men to dig out and build. Some are still employed by Amandari today, while other original staff have retired and their offspring hired.
The backstory
Aman’s founder, Adrian Zecha, is Indonesian, and a large percentage of his earliest property openings were in Bali and across the archipelago. (Today there are five resorts, plus a phinisi yacht.) Amandari came right on the heels of Phuket's Amanpuri, which opened in 1988. In quick succession, the ’90s saw the openings of Amankila and Aman Villas at Nusa Dua, Amanwana on Moyo Island, and Amanjiwo on Java.
I was told Australian architect Peter Muller had been noodling over his concept for a village-inspired resort for more than 15 years when he met with Zecha; they agreed it was perfect for the river gorge site outside Ubud. At the time, building just 30 rooms was a risky proposition, but the hotelier was willing to take a chance. There was plenty of buzz about the project as it was being built, from the novel swimming pool to the way Muller planned to keep the village path open to reach the important centuries-old tiger statue by the river. He intentionally split the lobby’s alang-alang roof in two to leave a six-foot opening for the twice-yearly processions that wind down 229 steps—a glute-burning yet worthwhile little journey to make in the mornings.
Though the architecture remains the same, the resort has evolved over the years. It still features the original indoor library, stacked with histories and tomes on Bali and Indonesia—there was no Wi-Fi, Google, or ChatGPT back then, after all, to answer guests’ queries. At first it was quite simple, sans spa and activities, but when guests began asking to take guided walks into the surrounding villages and rice fields, Amandari formally offered rice field trekking, which remains, in my experience, a must-do for an illuminating look at daily life and Bali’s UNESCO-recognized 9th-century subak irrigation system.
The rooms
I arrived during rainfall, creating a charming ambience but also plenty of puddles. Amandari is a rare Bali resort that doesn’t use golf carts to shuttle guests from place to place, and it makes a big difference in maintaining a strong sense of peace and quiet, allowing the songs of nature to dominate.
The heart of my Pool Suite—and all the suites in fact, which elsewhere might be called villas—was a perfect square encased in glass and massive panels that slide to cover the glass like blackout blinds, stretching up toward a gorgeous bamboo-framed Balinese-style pitched roof. This same structure is a common thread between suite types, which boast a variety of views and footprints, some with an additional indoor living room, lounge, and kitchen space. Amid all the lovely craftsmanship, something I really loved: There was no TV hanging on the wall, or anywhere for that matter. (They are available by request.)
The suites are all creams, woods, and accents of green. The very cozy super king bed feels regal thanks to a solid canopy hovering above it. Spacious, wood-paneled, yet light-filled describes the bathroom with double-sided vanities and closets, plus a sunken outdoor marble tub large enough for two—a hallmark of each suite—which can be cleaned and filled for you upon request. Tiny details were well considered: The cempaka flowers in the toilet room were a nice touch and I appreciated the constantly-burning mosquito-repelling incense and the weather-appropriate short, lightweight cotton bathrobe. Walking sticks with woven handles and a yoga mat made me want to move my body, which I found a subtle and welcome nudge.
I also appreciated the Bali-made snacks and beverages in the minibar (all complimentary, except for alcohol) that support local businesses. The pretty bowl of fresh fruit was changed daily and Diko, my dedicated butler, double-checked my dietary preferences before mentioning that the house-made date cookies could be switched to a gluten-free option.
I love a hotel that does something special at turndown, and Amandari delivered. On the carefully folded duvet I’d find a tale of local lore or Balinese bedtime story as well as a small handmade memento—a wooden mask carved by a disabled craftsman one night, an intricate palm-leaf representation of the rice goddess Dewi Sri another.
Another highlight of my suite type was a divine 26-foot-long swimming pool with lounge chairs and, in the spirit of comprehensive attention to detail, a pitcher of cold water, SPF 50, and cooling gel that sat on the dining table of my private bale, a small open-air pavilion with a roof and bamboo or wood floor. Each suite has one, regardless of whether it has a pool.
Food and drink
There’s but one restaurant here and it’s simply called The Restaurant. The open-air space serves breakfast and an all-day menu from the highest point at the resort, with a densely green panorama to match and roll-down screens for inclement weather. Half the menu is dedicated to Indonesian and Balinese dishes, favorites being a selection of satay and sambal kecap, Jimbaran-style fish, and falling-apart bebek betutu, a classic Bali take on duck. There’s plenty of spice to go around, especially in the form of chili-based sambal, but servers are more than happy to adjust the level of heat and diligently reconfirm allergies and dietary preferences at each meal. Some cocktails feature arak, the island’s fermented, distilled spirit, like the Taksu Ubud with tamarind and turmeric that was smooth and tangy; mocktails are readily available too.
Dishes from “neighboring borders”—Singapore, India, Thailand—feature as well, plus a page for plant-based dining and a further selection of international staples, from pizza and pasta to a cheeseburger. For lunch one day I went fully Mediterranean, and savored the very best halloumi of my life, charcoal-grilled and topped with raw forest honey, za’atar, pomegranate, pine nuts, and citrus. I also found the shawarma wrap—tucked adorably into a banana leaf and tied with a jute bow—a fantastic melody of flavors. Another highlight was charcoal-grilled line-caught Balinese octopus with Spanish seasoning and giant caper berries.
Breakfast, included in all stays, is something to look forward to, with a large selection of both Indonesian and Western dishes served in portions small enough that guests might try several in one sitting.
Tailored to the many couples, there are also multiple special dining options. These include a Ngejot Balinese Feast, which opens with a private Balinese traditional dance performance before a rijstaffel-style meal of island classics like babi guling (spit-roasted suckling pig) and sate lilit (minced fish or pork satay wrapped around lemongrass stalks).
The Terrace is where the resort’s bar—with gilded carved floral flourishes, plump rattan armchairs, and a hefty wooden chandelier—is found, busiest during the popular afternoon tea (complimentary for all resort guests). There is always Bali coffee (called kopi) and some type of herbal tea being poured, plus fresh fruit and several Balinese and Indonesian bites.
The spa
Amandari’s spa is small but bliss-inducing. Aman’s dreamy catalogue of signature treatments for the body and face are on the menu alongside locally inspired rituals. I tried the Mandi Rempah, meaning herbal bath, which began with a floral sea salt foot bath in one of the two outdoor couples’ rooms (there’s also an air-conditioned single room inside). Widi, my therapist, spent two hours laying on the TLC, first with a powerful yet gentle massage using super fragrant vanilla-infused sunflower oil, next rubbing a detoxifying local black rice and cinnamon scrub into my skin, and finally spreading on, in long sweeping strokes, a volcanic Balinese clay mask. To cleanse it all, I sat on the step of a steaming frangipani- and rose-filled bath, where she poured hot yogurt on me for good measure and then I soaked, sipping rosella tea and feeling like a princess.
In-house guests may book complimentary 30-minute sessions in the intimate sauna and steam room for groups up to four with an advance reservation. There are also tennis courts and a coach, an intimate but well-equipped fitness center atop a lotus pond, and complimentary hatha yoga classes that I found quite strong and opening, taught by a Balinese instructor three mornings a week.
The area
Lovely as it is on property, it would be a shame not to venture out when you're so close to Ubud’s acclaimed and bustling center known for art and textiles, yoga, shopping, and monkeys, not to mention dining, with quite a few celebrated restaurants in the area (Nusantara, Locavore NXT, Room4Dessert, Mozaic, and Hujan Locale, to name a few). Gushing waterfalls, the impressive Tegalalang rice terraces, and cooking classes also capture travelers’ attention. If crowds aren’t appealing, Amandari guests can opt to go with a tour guide to the nearby water temple Tirta Empul for an early-morning melukat purification ceremony before it opens to the public.
The service
Five-star all the way, with all the tiny details anticipated and personalized greetings by name. But this is also an island resort, and the staff manage to toe the line beautifully—there’s not a hint of stuffiness to be found, and actually a heartwarming casualness peeked through at times and felt appropriate for the location. In The Restaurant, I only experienced a couple of instances where service or drinks lagged a bit, however the gracious butlers were always quick to reply thoughtfully on WhatsApp.
A complimentary shuttle to the center of Ubud is possible at any time with 15 minutes notice, and the staff excel at tough reservations, such as a booking a seat at Room4Dessert with just one day’s notice.
For families
A prior visit with my then nine-month-old and three-year-old daughters revealed the genuine warmth of all the staff, from servers to gardeners. There are many child-friendly activities on offer, including crafting toys from banana stems, kite making, and Balinese dance class. With older kids, families have plenty to engage in, from rice field trekking (stunning but very sweaty, I will say, while wearing a baby) to river rafting and cycling excursions. All ages have fun making shadow puppets, called wayang, and watching a private 45-minute storytelling performance. Baby cribs and rollaway cots are available on request, and The Restaurant has wooden high chairs plus a solid kids’ menu.
Eco effort
Zero plastic, even for in-room dining, was very nice to see. The resort sources much of its ingredients locally, including its heritage rice from nearby Begawan Giri’s regenerative, chemical-free farm. The sustainability program to support Bali water protection is great in theory, but I forgot to place the card on my bed and when I returned from breakfast housekeeping had already stripped the bed, which I felt bad about since I certainly don’t require new sheets after just one night’s sleep.
Cultural sustainability is front of mind and has been since the beginning, when young Balinese dancers from Kedewatan were invited to practice each afternoon in a pavilion embraced by a lotus pond—one of them, all grown up, now works in reservations. Thirty six years later, during my stay, I joined in, attempting (and failing) to follow the young ladies’ tari pendet dance with the help of their instructor moving me like a marionette doll, and having a great time doing it.
Accessibility
Bali has never been a very accessible destination and this resort has just one suite with a couple of stairs that staff can cover with a ramp. However, reaching most everything else, including The Restaurant, requires climbing steps.
Anything left to mention?
It’s not awash with dining and drinking options, but one can’t help but feel the soul of Amandari is its most irreplaceable asset. In the longstanding staff, the dancing village girls, and the turndown crafts, there is an authenticity, an
actually
made-in-Bali-ness that is wonderfully pure and captures the spirit of traveling to new places to discover what they’re really all about. Here, it feels like the essence of Bali distilled.
