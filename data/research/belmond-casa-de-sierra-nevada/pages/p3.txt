Casa de Sierra Nevada, A Belmond Hotel, San Miguel de Allende
An unquestionably sensory hotel experience

Reviewed by
Betsy Blumenthal
Why book?
Very rarely does a hotel get everything right—particularly the ones that have to do the delicate dance between preserving the history of their spaces with the conveniences of modern life. But Belmond’s Casa de Sierra Nevada does it with aplomb, firmly cementing its status as the belle of
San Miguel de Allende
.
Set the scene?
The hotel comprises seven buildings in San Miguel de Allende’s historic Centro district, six of which are meant to house guests—and all of which date back to the 17th and 18th centuries. But they’re not as likely to catch your attention as they are to surprise you. (Buildings in the neighborhood are barred from erecting any exterior major signage, so as to preserve the integrity of the façades and the area’s old-world charm). Take its main complex on Hospicio Street: Behind the building’s imposing wooden doors, a whole world unfurls. A dark entryway paved with worn stone leads to a bright, airy courtyard with a ceiling aperture that opens and closes with the weather; along the walls, thick patches of ivy nearly threaten to overtake wrought-iron lanterns, and potted palm fronds wave in the breeze. On the ruddy ceramic tile floor, polished-up wooden tables—seating for the hotel’s main restaurant Andanza—are arranged just so, and seated at them, clusters of the hotel’s stylish guests, taking their meals.
The crowd
It’s a fairly limited crowd: Well-heeled Mexican nationals on a weekend trip with family, and Americans (mostly retired, or in their 30s but without kids) with money to spend. These aren’t flashy folks. What they’re after is a sense of seclusion and refinement, but aren’t keen on big, ostentatious displays of luxury.
The rooms
I stayed across the street from the main house, in a building that was once a monastery. Entering into the complex from the street felt like stumbling upon some secret garden: through high wooden doors, you’d suddenly find yourself enshrined in stone walls, with a fountain gently bubbling at the center of it all. My suite, 445, was magnificent. It felt rustic but regal, elegant but comfortable. The rich, dark wooden furniture and spare white walls offset the room’s few pops of color, rendered mostly in blue—geometric patterned curtains, the couch and twinned wingback chairs, the edges of a carpet, the lamps, while the built-ins surrounding the sitting room TV displayed an artful smattering of bric-a-brac (coffee table books and painted ceramic plates and vases) that gave the space a sense of warmth. When I returned from my jaunts around town each day, it really felt like I was coming home to somewhere comfortable and inviting, rather than delicate, sterile, or foreign. Every evening, while soaking off the day in my tub, I’d listen to the toll of the nearby church bells and the honks of the few cars that trudged up and down the street. But even those noises faded into nothing once the wooden shutters were closed at turndown, and the soft whir of the air conditioning took over.
Most magnificent, though, was the private rooftop connected to my room, which only I could access through a spiral staircase in the walled vestibule beside my sitting room. At the top, I had not only a 360-degree view of San Miguel, but a small table and chairs; two chaise longues; and, as if that weren’t enough, my very own hot tub. My days and nights were packed, so I never got to use it—and I’m still kicking myself.
Food and drink
One of the things that I looked forward to most each day was breakfast at Andanza, the main restaurant, which also has a handful of tables clustered in a separate room beside the courtyard. Fresh pots of drip coffee, cappuccino, espresso—all were perfectly executed, and served piping hot. Among the standouts: the seasonal fruit plate, stacked with papaya, cantaloupe, pineapple, served with a small pot of yogurt and a just-sweet-enough homemade granola (I loved the unexpected pops of puffed amaranth) and the local tomato toast, a piece of crisped rye layered with slices of the fruit in various preparations—grilled, confited, sundried—along with melted manchego cheese, balsamic glaze, and basil.
Dinner, too, was a pleasure at Andanza—it’s the most formal of the hotel’s three dining venues—but I also loved nibbling on Peruvian-inflected tapas at Tunki Rooftop by Handshake, which hovers just above the courtyard with the pool. One of my favorite bites was probably the grilled watermelon tiradito; Chef Andres Guerra masterminded the dish, whose flavor and texture is meant to mimic tuna tataki, by sous-viding a hunk of the fruit. Dreamt up by Handshake, one of Mexico City’s reigning cocktail bars, the mixed drinks were a welcome complement to the food—and though both were assertive in flavor and preparation, neither one overtook the other. (For my part, I sipped on a steady stream of coconut and pandan negronis, which achieved just the right balance of sweet and bitter.)
Tell us about the spa
I had a signature Laja massage—what I genuinely believe to have been one of the best of my life—at the spa. There’s just something about the way the experience feels when the masseuse has a truly attuned sense of touch, and they’re able to adjust to your needs without much prompting. There are just three treatment rooms, in addition to a small boutique and locker rooms that are really meant for one person at a time—but the size of it made the experience feel intimate and secluded. After my treatment, I posted up on the enclosed terrace overlooking the skyline with a glass of bright-pink hibiscus water, and watched the sun set.
I didn’t have a chance to take a dip in the small but lovely pool, which was ensconced in a courtyard connected to mine, but I wish I’d made time for it. One thing I did have time for, though, was the private cooking class at Sazón, for which I was joined by head chef Ruben and sous-chef Alejandra. Over five hours, we made tamales and a number of other traditional Mexican dishes—and between prep and dining, they guided me through the nearby market to hand-select fresh ingredients.
The neighborhood
One of the hotel’s principal draws is its location. Set in the heart of Zona Centro, Casa de Sierra Nevada’s seven buildings are embedded deep in the warren of the neighborhood’s narrow, cobblestone streets, with many of the city’s main attractions only a few minutes’ stroll away: the Mercado de Artesanías, where you’re as likely to stumble upon a produce vendor hawking cherimoya and sapote as you are a craftsperson displaying their works of molded tin.
The service
Thoughtful, eager, smooth. I got to know—or at the very least, have prolonged conversations with—many members of the hotel staff during the course of my stay, whether sitting at the bar for a drink or grabbing breakfast in the sunlit courtyard of Andanza. But those chats never felt obligatory or strenuous, and if I didn’t at least learn something new and interesting about the person with whom I was speaking, then I at least left the conversation with a genuine sense of warmth. As a person traveling alone, I was checked on frequently, but discreetly—I was attended to, but not smothered.
By way of example, I’d point to the handful of personalized items that were purchased from the local crafts market and placed in my room just for me: I was greeted almost immediately by a picture of me and my husband from our wedding, printed and ensconced in a tin frame and placed on the bedside table, and a colorfully embroidered pillow, also stitched with my name in light pink. One afternoon, I came home to a pair of textured paper bookmarks placed gingerly atop my stack of books. Little details really made the space feel like home, and made me feel like I was being looked after.
Is there anything you'd change?
The worst thing about San Miguel de Allende was getting there. I had to fly from Newark to Houston, then Houston to Leon, followed by an hour-and-a-half-long car ride to the hotel. Each way netted out to about 13 hours of travel. It was, in a word, exhausting. But after that, everything was gravy.
