Aria Hotel Prague
A charming luxury boutique hotel inspired by music.

Reviewed by
Sophie Friedman
Why book?
If you have a soft spot for classical music, crushed velvet, and fewer tourists, this is the spot for you. Just across the Charles Bridge from Prague’s positively heaving Old Town, and within the city’s UNESCO Historic Center, the music-themed Aria (get it?) Prague is well-positioned for sightseeing while being removed enough from the cacophony of Old Town to make for a relaxing stay.
Set the scene
The guests at Aria Prague come from dozens of mostly European countries, and their ages span decades, but what they all have in common is wanting to stay in a hotel with character. This is an Old World hotel with a music theme—the buildings date from the early 18th and late 19th centuries, the curtains are thick, the settees in crushed velvet, the club chairs in leather, the color of cognac. There are cut-glass vases of flowers and lots of throw pillows in the winter garden atrium, and the dining room is lit by a chandelier, its tables dressed in white tablecloths even at breakfast. And, of course, there’s background music being played in the public spaces, everything from a Dvořák piano concerto to Taylor Swift, all at a pleasant volume.
The motley mix of guests is not as formal; guests are here on vacation, wearing Birkenstocks and comfortable walking shoes in warmer weather, along with light sweaters and jeans during the cooler months (during Prague’s frigid winter, the hotel has very low occupancy).
Aria Prague is a hotel where you’ll definitely see the other guests. The rooms are plenty comfortable, but the ground floor is designed to be a third-space—there’s the light-filled winter garden atrium, a small library with cognac-colored club chairs, a fireplace, and 20 red bookshelves stacked with music books; and another small library. The latter is packed with thousands of DVDs and CDs for guests to borrow, everything from Bocelli to Bartok, Chick Corea to Billie Holiday. When was the last time you even held a CD? It’s all part of the hotel’s charm.
The backstory
Three historic buildings were renovated and joined by a glass atrium in 2003 to form Aria. At one point, the buildings were a printing press, and the long entry to the lobby is where trucks would pull in to unload reams of paper. The hotel was originally owned by Library Hotel Collection and had a sister outpost in Budapest.
The rooms
The 51 rooms are spread across four floors, each with a music theme—contemporary, classical, opera, or jazz. Rooms face either the inner courtyard, the road, or Vrtba Garden, and all are very quiet. The walls of the buildings are thick, and within each room are heavy drapes and carpeting that muffle any sounds.
Crushed velvet slipper chairs and settees in blush and Baltic blue are a welcome perch after a day of pounding Prague’s cobblestone streets. The music theme is carried through each room, but it’s subtle enough; quarter music notes on the carpet, record grooves on the armoire and desk. And to listen to music, yours or the hotel's, you can connect to Apple TV, which plays through Marantz amplifiers.
We slept very well on the firm mattress, but the hotel has toppers available for guests who prefer a bit more cushioning. In July, the sun rises in Czechia around 4:50 a.m., but the hotel’s two layers of blackout shades and curtains do the trick.
In each room and throughout the hallways are framed caricatures of musicians, running from Igor Stravinsky and Pavarotti to Bob Dylan and Louis Armstrong, but they were almost all men. We were disappointed to see so few female musicians represented.
Food and drink
A breakfast buffet is set up in the light-filled winter garden with all you need to start the day—baskets of croissants, dark and white breads, fruit, crudites, boiled eggs, and the pièce de résistance, some of the best yogurt we’ve ever had (and that’s saying a lot), from a nearby farm with whom the hotel works. Fill your plate, then order à la carte, an omelet, say, or palačinky (Czech crepes, served here with jam or Nutella)
The hotel’s Michelin-listed fine dining restaurant, Coda, serves guests pan-Continental fare either in the intimate formal dining room (where you can have breakfast) or on the rooftop. Eat indoors and the experience is more formal: the room has damask wallpaper, chinoiserie lamps, a large, gilded chandelier. On our visit, the rooftop wasn’t yet serving food, and as the dining room has no windows, it can feel a bit oppressive, and so we opted to eat elsewhere.
A sweet afternoon tea, included in the room rate, is served from 4-5 p.m.; in the warmer months, this switches to prosecco with savory snacks.
The neighborhood/area
The cobblestone streets of Malá Strana, Prague’s (rather-unfairly-named) Lesser Town are just as charming as those across the Charles Bridge in Prague’s Old Town—with far fewer study-abroad students and drunken bachelor and bachelorette parties in sight. Praguers live here, and you’ll see them jogging or walking dogs in Petrin Gardens.
Aria is in a prime position for sightseeing throughout the UNESCO-protected historic center, either on foot or by catching the tram just two minutes away. One of the loveliest sites is right outside the back door; the hotel has a private entrance to Vrtba Garden, an exceptional Italian-style Baroque terrace-garden dating to 1715. Prague Castle and its spacious grounds are 10 minutes’ walk from the hotel.
Of course, we’re not telling you to eschew Old Town, with its concentration of gobsmacking Gothic and art nouveau buildings, but it feels nice to come back to a calm hotel at the end of the day.
The service
The hotel staff are really, genuinely warm. There are only 51 rooms at Aria, so even when full, there are scarcely 100 guests, nothing for seasoned hotel pros. Ask for suggestions on where to see art nouveau architecture, inquire about somewhere to watch the Olympics, mention Prague Spring—they’re game for conversation.
For families
Although this is a rather grown-up hotel, it’s not unfriendly to older kids, especially those with an interest in music. Comfortable public spaces like the winter garden and libraries mean the whole family isn’t trapped in one or two rooms. The Mozart and Beethoven two-bedroom suites each accommodate up to four people—one queen and two twins—and the Dvorak and Blues suites have king and sofa beds for up to three people. Families looking for an evening activity after the sun goes down can make good use of the hotel’s smaller, six-seat cinema and its hundreds of DVDs.
Accessibility
The hotel’s public spaces are all wheelchair accessible, and there are two accessible rooms. Cabs can pull right up outside the hotel, which is on a flat street, and there are no obstacles between the entrance and the elevator.
Anything left to mention?
The fifth-floor rooftop is a perfect little hideaway: guests enjoy a panoramic view of Prague’s red-tiled rooftops, but because this terrace space doesn’t face the street, it feels like a secret. Open April to October, its smattering of wooden tables shaded by orange umbrellas gives guests a front-row view of the oxidized dome of the Baroque St. Nicholas Church and, in the distance, the towers of Prague Castle.
