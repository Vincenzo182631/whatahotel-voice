Nujuma, a Ritz-Carlton Reserve, Saudi Arabia
Surrounded by pristine coral on a little-visited Red Sea archipelago off Saudi Arabia, the new Nujuma resort reflects both untapped nature and hyperreal modernity

Reviewed by
Noo Saro-Wiwa
Why book Nujuma, a Ritz-Carlton Reserve?
Nujuma (meaning “stars” in Arabic) is the latest opening under the Red Sea Global project. Located just off
Saudi Arabia
’s west coast in the Ummahat Islands, it’s one of the most unexploited and unvisited archipelagos on Earth. At this resort there’s a chance to explore Saudi culture, cuisine and nature while chilling in or diving in some of the most pristine coral reefs. With secluded villas and first-rate restaurants aided by international, forward-facing staff, Nujuma feels like a digestible entrée to the kingdom, in a Red Sea region that feels primed to give
the Maldives
a run for their money.
Set the scene
There’s a futuristic, otherworldly quality to the overwater villas, which are spread out on a circular walkway elevated above pristine white sands lapped by water of such cerulean intensity it looks filtered. Families, couples and
honeymooners
fly into Red Sea International Airport and transfer by seaplane or yacht from Red Sea Global’s seaport to Nujuma. Wearing anything from shorts and vests to niqabs (the dress code is laissez-faire), they drain their tensions on beachside cabanas, watch the waves from villa terraces, cycle along overwater causeways, or stargaze at night while sipping mocktails and puffing shisha. The only sound is the near-constant breeze that keeps annual temperatures at 32˚C all year round. A strong sense of quiet seclusion and privacy prevails here – when I spotted a global celebrity, my chances of laying eyes on them again were slim.
Alongside the cosmopolitan and international staff, modern and traditional Saudi culture is quietly present throughout, from the artwork, artefacts and books at Conservation House, to the cuisine and alcohol-free drinks menu.
The backstory
When Marriott Bonvoy launched Nujuma – its first ever Ritz-Carlton Reserve in the Middle East – it joined the St Regis on the uninhabited Ummahat Islands as the latest luxury resort in Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea Global project, which aims to diversify the kingdom’s economy and expand its tourism sector. Doors opened in May 2024.
The rooms
Nujuma has 63 solar-powered, one- to three-bedroom villas, twenty of which are built over the water and connected by an eye-catching circular elevated walkway. Designed by Foster + Partners and inspired by seashells, these spheroid structures are at once natural yet futuristic. Inside, the ceiling arcs above an open-plan living space, kitchen and telescope for nighttime stargazing. The decor is accented with locally crafted artefacts, carvings and design motifs, such as the black and white cushions, in a nod to the Al Sadu weaving technique used by Bedouins. My rear wooden lounge terrace and infinity pool overlook the water and the beachside villas beyond, where the sun sinks or rises (depending on your villa position) between dunes and swaying grass reeds. Each villa has a dedicated personal host who can handle all your requests and transport you anywhere on a buggy; alternatively, you can cycle along the elevated causeways.
Food and drink
Former Michelin star chef Lucas Julien-Vauzelle oversees the food at Nujuma’s four restaurants. At the seafood restaurant, Tabrah (meaning “luckiest catch”), Julien-Vauzelle brings a French influence to Arab cuisine. Red Sea tuna carpaccio with stracciatella and caviar, or baked lobster with Mornay sauce precede Arabic rice pudding with mango sourced from the nearby town of Umluj. Zero-alcohol mocktails like the
Shipshape To Nihon Retto
(sea buckthorn “spirit”, grapefruit, lemongrass, yuzu and ginger) do an excellent job of tricking my palate.
For breakfast, I headed to my favourite restaurant, Sita, a Levantine eatery and French-style patisserie where the stunning outdoor pavilions have arched woven-wood ceilings that mimic traditional leaf weaving. Arab breakfast dishes, seasoned from an extensive spice library, include scrambled egg and sujuk sausages, and mahalabiya – milk pudding and mango. Arabic bread is made fresh from a traditional clay oven, and there are also French pastries: the chocolatine pain perdu slathered with grapefruit jam tasted like heaven. In the alfresco Jamaa restaurant, guests can order mezzes, salads, pizzas and other light dishes prepared over a wood-fire grill close to the outdoor pool and beach cabanas. Maia does mocktails such as The Sun House Sipper (Crossip hibiscus, lyre’s spiced cane, karkadeh, vanilla, lemon and mint).
Activities
The resort’s Galaxea and Wama clubs offer sailing, windsurfing, scuba diving, snorkelling, kayaking and paddleboarding. The islands are clustered in one of several “blue holes” – underwater sinkholes – in the southern Red Sea, an untouched part of the world’s fourth-largest barrier reef system. Needless to say, the coral here is unspoiled, which means snorkelers and scuba divers are super-spoiled, getting up close to 165 reef fish species, dugongs, giant stingrays, spinner dolphins, plus hawksbill turtles. At the house reef, my Kenyan snorkelling instructor, Msanzu Baya, pointed out a puffer fish hovering over a brain coral amongst Arabian angelfish, Oriental sweet lips, a Napoleon wrasse and a giant stingray, which vanished a few seconds later in a shimmer of bubbles.
Back on land, the American resident naturalist Sean Laughlin brings this landscape alive with his infectious enthusiasm. During guided walks among the mangroves, he’ll point out halavi guitarfish, blue-spotted fantail rays, green turtles, the Eurasian Hoopoe and the island’s three hawksbill nests. New species are being discovered all the time, Laughlin says. Excursions to the desert dunes on the coastal mainland can also be arranged.
Conservation House is an elegant and evolving cultural space dedicated to connecting guests with Saudi culture and traditions. Lining its walls are local artefacts, sculptures and books ranging from falconry to mosque architecture. And perched on the tables are matryoshka-esque dolls, hand-painted by artists to showcase the colourful clothing of the various regions. Manager Ohoud Mosuily is available for coffee and informal chats about anything from the kingdom’s 18th-century origins to Jeddah’s modern coffee culture.
After-dinner stargazing on the beach taps into Saudi’s astrology and storytelling tradition. The light pollution doesn’t make for the darkest skies, but I enjoy lying back on a pouffe in the warm breeze as astrologer Habib Bafeil wields his laser pen at the sky and recounts an ancient folk tale about the Thurayya, or Seven Sisters, cluster.
Spa
Therapists at the Neyrah Spa deploy Voya products and various techniques from around the world to deliver a range of bespoke therapies, including meditation, sound healing, lunar yoga and mud baths. There are vitality and lap pools, and a fitness centre. In the hammam I’m scrubbed down so vigorously it feels as if the impurities are scoured from my body, mind and soul.
The service
The service is friendly and deferential. Some of the English-speaking staff, who come from 46 countries, have previously worked in hospitality in the Middle East.
Eco effort
The resort is 100 per cent solar powered, but there are backup generators. Reverse osmosis turns seawater into freshwater, and the subsequent grey water is treated at Nujuma’s own sewage treatment plant before being used for plant irrigation.
For kids
There’s a playground for children, and there are plans to offer nature tours and activities for the young ones.
Accessibility
All spaces are at ground level, and buggies can transport you everywhere.
