JW Marriott Hotel Tokyo
A surprisingly tranquil sanctuary for busy Tokyo travelers.

Reviewed by
Megan Spurrell
Why book?
In Tokyo's new Takanawa Gateway City—a well-connected development in Minato City—this new JW Marriott outpost is a surprisingly tranquil sanctuary for busy travelers.
Set the scene
To put this in New York City terms—rarely useful for understanding Tokyo, but bear with me—Takanawa Gateway City is a bit like Hudson Yards. It's a neighborhood seemingly magicked out of thin air (or, more literally, available space on the fringes of the city's business center), into which tons of dollars and new infrastructure have been poured in recent years. This means new shops (from Muji to Blue Bottle coffee), restaurants, and efficient transportation are right at the foot of the hotel, which occupies a number of floors in a high-rise building called The Link. Because the hotel is right on two Japan Railways lines (just two stops from Tokyo Station, and easily situated on the Yamanote line for heading northwest to areas like Ebisu, Shinjuku, etc.; you'll also see the Shinkansen bullet train snaking past), I arrived by train and wheeled right into the building, where an elevator whisked me up a couple dozen stories to the high-rise lobby. There, honeymooners and businessfolk alike will be spotted at the check-in desk, or taking the spiral staircase down to the breakfast buffet.
The backstory
For JW Marriott fans, and particularly those who care about their Marriott Bonvoy points, the brand's entry into Japan is an exciting one. Two JW Marriotts have recently opened in the country (first in Nara, opened in 2020, then here in October 2025). In keeping with the brand's global ethos, they focus on pure comfort, slinky settings, and at-your-service attendance, with the privacy and serenity that most Tokyo visitors will crave after soaking up the city's energy.
The rooms
Moody, low-lift hallways guide visitors to the rooms, the defining feature of which is a huge window looking out upon the sprawl of the city. Wood paneling from the doors to the armoire to even the ceiling lends a warmth to the space, punctuated by glossy acrylic and velvet in tones of grey. The room has a global feel—nothing too distinctly screams of Tokyo—though the chance to see Mount Fuji peeking through the clouds from some rooms will certainly ground you in the destination. To me, the feeling is somewhat like being in a cocoon, not that it's particularly small (though, like most city hotels, there isn't room to do yoga in here), but in that you leave the bright lights and noise of the city for this softer, dimmer spaces, where your senses can reset for a moment before you fling yourself back into the city. One word to the wise: There isn't much room for luggage in the current setup. We struggled to fit an open carry-on on the in-closet ledge (it had to be closed whenever we wanted to slide the door shut), and we didn't have another space for the second one. If you're traveling with checked bags, consider asking about a fold-up rack or additional space at check-in.
Food and drink
The breakfast buffet, lavish in volume but also variety, should be your first sign that you will eat well here. Diners are offered a bento box for a traditional Japanese breakfast, and in addition to proteins like salmon as a centerpiece, you can also choose from Western plates like Eggs Benedict. The buffet, for its part, is a complement to this all, with a fridge full of fresh juices and fruits (pick one and it'll be taken to the kitchen and sliced for you), baskets overflowing with breads, and both Western staples like smoked salmon and charcuterie, plus Japanese dishes like fresh rice, miso soup, and an assortment of pickles and steamed vegetables. Come dinner, Kakō offers seasonal ingredients in a tasting menu format, while Saki is an eight-seat experience led by Michelin-starred chefs. Sefino, a Mediterranean spot, has since opened, and there are other outposts like a cafe with French pastries and coffee in the lobby, and, of course, the JW Bar for something stronger. I visited within a couple weeks of opening, when Kakō was a bit quiet and finding its feet on the vibe front, though every dish delivered. As the crowd swells, I imagine outposts (particularly Saki and Sefino with name-recognizable chefs) will draw in diners beyond hotel guests.
The spa
The indoor pool area is fabulous, with both a hot tub and swimming pool, as well as sweeping views of the city. No photos are allowed, much like in Japanese onsen, which frankly allows you to settle into the serenity of the space. Likewise, the fitness center—decked out in floor-to-ceiling wood for soothing ryokan vibes—has top-notch gear, and thoughtful details like headphones in case you forgot yours. I didn't make it to the spa itself, though guests who do can look forward to services like massages and scalp treatments.
The neighborhood/area
There's a lot of construction outside—not prohibitively so, but you can feel that this is an area on the move. It's incredibly conveniently located if you plan to take the Shinkansen to other destinations in Japan, and I found myself hopping on the Yamanote line to head just about everywhere I wanted to go. I did notice that I was always traveling about 30-plus minutes in the same direction to explore neighborhoods to the northwest (some even an hour away), but being on such a core train line was helpful. And it isn't that there aren't things outside your door: I was able to run to the Imperial Palace, and find casual izakaya and soba restaurants within walking distance. But like any neighborhood coming (or being built) into its own, it didn't tend to appear on any of the recs I was given from friends. It was a perfect launchpad, but not quite the destination itself.
The service
The team is incredibly available to help, but manages to slip out of sight when they aren't needed.
Accessibility
This is a big, elevator-accessible building.
Anything left to mention?
Don't miss breakfast!
