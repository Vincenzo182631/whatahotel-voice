First in: Casa Baglioni, Milan
A mid-century Milanese townhouse is the sophisticated new stay in town.

Why book Casa Baglioni?
Because in a city known for flashy hotels, this is a luxe place to bed down that’s (successfully) setting itself apart from the likes of the Armani and Bulgari brand of stays. This is the Baglioni brand’s follow-up to its now-closed Carlton across town; but rather than try to play the grand-dame properties at their own game this place has leaned into its artsy neighborhood and townhouse-like structure, as well as a midcentury aesthetic that’s synonymous with Milan.
Set the scene
If you didn’t have your eyes peeled, you might just miss Casa Baglioni as you walked the streets of Brera, the city’s art district. Brera is no stranger to extravagant hotels—Armani, Bulgari, and Mandarin Oriental fringe this neighborhood’s boundaries—but this is a fresh take. When you enter this townhouse hotel, you’re greeted first by the neon-strip swirly chandelier, a nod to the Lucio Fontana-designed light fittings at Museo del Novecento, a 20th-century museum that houses a clutch of the mid-century artists the hotel’s design draws on. It’s a manifesto for the rest of the hotel’s carefully curated art scene; think Gio Ponti-inspired fabrics and art.
The backstory
The Baglioni brand has been around in Milan for a long time. But its first hotel here, the Carlton, was a pandemic casualty, closing its doors a few years ago. It wasn’t long before the Polito crew— the founding family of Baglioni properties—were eyeing up a new spot in the city. This 1910s-built townhouse once belonged to fashion designer Philipp Plein, and was the perfect, refined space for Baglioni to take over. It opened in early spring 2023; with more to come (including a rooftop bar that promises widescreen views) throughout the next few years.
The rooms
Fairly pared back, as smart Italian hotels go. For a country that loves to show off its frescoes and statues and fashion designers, this is a stripped-back iteration of Italian decor—one you have to learn more about to truly appreciate. Rooms are all chestnut wood with curves to die for and artful shelving; midcentury sofas and armchairs and books singing the praises of Milanese art and design. The bathrooms are really something; freestanding tubs lend some drama while a rainfall shower provides practicality—these are bathrooms that are designed to spend time in, not utilitarian spaces. Larger rooms are cleverly zoned using chrome dividers and distinct-height ceilings; the nicest have terraces that overlook the leafy streets below. Book a higher floor for a better view; we like the west side of the building.
Food and drink
Claudio Sadler, the hotel’s big-name chef, is Milan born and bred. He agreed to close his very well-regarded restaurant next to the canals and move the whole, Michelin-starred operation to the ground floor of Casa Baglioni. His cooking is particularly Milanese; even on the tasting menu, you get a hint of the city’s unsung food culture: risotto Milanese, veal, and local cheese. There’s also an extensive wine cellar and a pretty private dining room. Later in 2023, a rooftop bar with views of the Duomo will open—we’d bet on it quickly becoming the place to be seen on sunny days in the city.
The neighborhood
Milan often gets a bad rep compared to the likes of Rome and Florence. Even the locals joke about the city being too corporate and rushed; a wholly un-Italian way of being. Still, the hotel is in Brera—Milan’s design district, where leafy, narrow streets are lined with arching trees, cute boutiques, artist studios, and sweet restaurants where people eat and drink outside year-round. The team at Casa Baglioni can arrange experiences that get under the skin of this bohemian neighborhood—a private tour of the local museum, a sneak peek at the art school, a visit to an authentic artists’ studio. It’s a fabulous way to go beyond Milan’s usual must-visits; these tours with private guides really aim to show guests a side of the city that even most Italians might deny exists: artsy, boho, and rooted in the past.
Eco effort
This was once the studio of fashion designer Falegnameria Cavalleroni, who installed waste-water systems and forward-thinking insulation that has lasted until today.
Accessibility
There are accessible elevators on several floors, and the restaurant and rooftop bar are fully accessible.
Is it worth it?
For a stay that will take its time in showing you the city like a local, this place can’t be beaten. Brera is easily the nicest neighborhood in Milan, and Casa Baglioni is fighting hard to be known as the dreamiest hotel here—in a slow, subtle, midcentury Milanese sort of way. It’s surprising but worth getting to know—much like Milan itself.
