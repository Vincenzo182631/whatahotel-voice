A London landmark reimagined

Reviewed by

Why book?
For a glimpse inside what was once one of
London
's most fortified buildings.
Set the scene
I've walked past this mammoth stone façade, on Grosvenor Square, a thousand times before—but I've never really
looked
at it. As the former American Embassy, that was
sort of
the point. This was one of the city's most impenetrable buildings, surrounded by armed guards, made to look uninviting and unappealing. Now, as I approach a few days after The Chancery Rosewood opens, the sunlight hits the gleaming stone and gold accents (added by Rosewood), drawing my attention from across the square. Door staff in biscuit-colored suits are greeting intrigued passersby who crane their necks for a look inside the lobby, inviting them to take a look around, and diners are sipping wine on the umbrella-strewn terrace at street level. Above this merry tableau, Theodore Roszak's 35-foot gilded eagle—an original feature from the building's Embassy era—watches over the square from the newly added seventh floor.
The backstory
Since the late 18th century, this corner of Mayfair has been tied to notable Americans. First, President John Adams lived here when he was the first United States Minister; as did James Monroe, John Quincy Adams, Martin Van Buren, and James Buchanan. Just before World War II, the Chancery relocated to 1 Grosvenor Square, across the street, before moving to this block-long building—six floors above ground, three below—in 1960. For almost 60 years, this was one of the city's least inviting landmarks, with increasingly stringent security deterring even the most placid of curious onlookers. When the Embassy moved to Nine Elms in 2018, Londoners buzzed about who and what might move in in its stead. In 2021, Hong Kong-based hotel group Rosewood announced plans to renovate the property to become its second London opening, joining the ranks of Rosewood London in Holborn. Four years later, following works which stripped the Grade-II listed Chancery back to its bare bones before adding layers of texture, art, and historical nods (plus three brand-new floors, made possible by the discovery of the original architect plans which included them), The Chancery Rosewood opened its doors. Through which I head, up the stairs and into the mid-century lobby, dominated by a huge, swirly chandelier hanging from the central atrium, to be swept smilingly to my room.
The rooms
Or rather, suite. This is an all-suite
hotel
—there are 144 of them, which feels like a relatively diminutive number (Rosewood London has 262 rooms, for context) and speaks to their size before I even set foot in one. Mine is on the sixth floor, with views of Grosvenor Square from the floor-to-ceiling windows on all three sides. Textures, rather than gilding, do a lot of the talking here—plush carpets my heels sink into as I snap a sneaky video to send to my curious commissioning editor, heavy-draped curtains, a velvet coffee table so long I could lie on it (I don't). I also have a study with a curvy sofa big enough to host my entire family for a cup of tea, and a sturdy desk. It strikes me as funny that everything in my suite is appropriately (American-ly) super-sized—from the TV screens in every room to the marble bath—but I'm not complaining: The vast proportions make this one of the most sumptuous suites I've seen in London.
Food and drink
There will, eventually, be eight restaurants and bars spread across the floors here, although not all are open when I visit. They will include a sushi spot from Tobi Masa, a chic space for afternoon tea, to a soundtrack of tinkling piano music, Jacqueline, which sits just off the lobby. During our stay, I dined at Serra—lots of curvy lines installed by New York-based designers AvroKO make the ever-blocky space warm and inviting, and the menu spotlights Southern Mediterranean crowd-pleasers: pillowy ricotta gnocchi, blushing steak, flatbreads with lardo. After supper, we're whisked up to get a glimpse of The Chancery Rosewood's trump card: the Eagle Bar. As the name might suggest, this newly added late-night terrace atop the building is dominated by that 35-foot gilded eagle, and is quickly becoming Mayfair's hottest table to book.
The neighborhood
Grosvenor Square was once known as Little America, but if you know where to look, there are countless cultures to discover in Mayfair. For foodies, some of our favorite restaurants in the neighborhood include Santiago Lastra's Mexican Fonda, slickly spiced hangout Koyn Thai, Punjabi hotspot Ambassadors' Clubhouse, and art-filled pub dining room Mount Street Restaurant. Here, you're also steps away from some of London's best shopping, on Bond Street and Oxford Street (Selfridge's is a short walk away).
The spa
I'm not much for spas or spa treatments, but the Asaya Spa, on one of the basement levels, has a lot to recommend itself. Firstly, it has—I'm told—the only full-length swimming pool in a Mayfair hotel, which means water babies can get a proper swim in during their stay here. There's also a chic 24-hour gym, sauna, and steam room. I book in for a bespoke facial—I leave feeling not just glowy and plumped, but with a list of skincare tips and the knots in my shoulders worked out, too.
The service
The staff I encountered all seemed genuinely excited to be working in such a landmark opening – and, indeed, that it was finally open at all. Check-in staff were accommodating and chatty, nothing was too much trouble for my butler, who guided me to the spa as the direct lift wasn't up and running yet, and made me a coffee as I settled into my suite. The service in Serra was efficient and personable, while in The Eagle Bar, our mixologist masterfully walked the line between professional and cheeky, giving us a sense of real ease.
Is it worth it?
This is a London landmark, reimagined—you won't regret seeing it for yourself.
