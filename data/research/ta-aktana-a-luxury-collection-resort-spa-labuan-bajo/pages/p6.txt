Reviewed by
Kathryn Romeyn
Why book?
Stretching from an emerald hilltop to the sapphire Flores Sea, Ta'aktana is a utopian Indonesian beach resort with striking accommodations on land and water that’s as dynamic as its location, embodying the intersection of ancient landscapes and wildlife—Komodo dragons to be exact—with the engrained, idiosyncratic culture of the West Manggarai Regency through resplendent design, colorful experiences, cogent culinary concepts, and a blissful spa.
Set the scene
It was a mere five scenic minutes—one and a half miles—after walking out of Komodo International Airport on the west end of Flores island that my driver pulled up to a grand, airy volume where several smiling staff stood waving like long-lost friends awaiting our arrival. I felt embraced instantly, drawn in by a flurry of enthusiastic greetings (for not just me but my infant daughter), delicious sips, welcome rituals and a green woven bracelet being tied to my wrist, its color representing the fertile land around us. It took only a few steps into the limestone-walled lobby with its wave-inspired chandelier and nearly life-sized brass canoe sculpture holding Botero-like figures for me to recognize the gobsmacking beauty of where we found ourselves, at the edge of Komodo National Park. Magnetically pulled toward the wide opening at the other end, I walked closer as the panorama zoomed in to reveal dozens of boats dotting shimmery blue water and conical islands plus the nearly 40–acre resort’s architecturally engaging structures rolling down to meet the layered seascape. It was a vista that somehow became more stunning every time I peeked, and one I compulsively photographed as the nuances of light and swell shifted.
This 2024 opening of Marriott International’s first hotel in the region—and a perfectly sized, thoughtfully designed, and glamorous one at that—acts as an invitation for a collection of worldly, curious and loyalty point–collecting young families, couples and travel buddies from around Asia and, increasingly, the west to immerse, not only in the mythical dragon experience but the deeper layers of artisanship, cuisine and personality that make make this a wildly worthy place to dig into Indonesia beyond Bali.
The backstory
For an owner who is passionate about the rich cultural heritage and natural beauty of Flores, plus harbors a personal connection to the underwater environment, Marriott’s The Luxury Collection is a fine fit, since the brand is known for embedding its properties in distinctive locations with sensitivity and respect. This second Luxury Collection resort in Indonesia marks the first time an international hotel brand has entered the Komodo region (IHG is set to be next, with a Crowne Plaza under construction), on an island whose name means “flowers” in Portuguese. Flores was under Portuguese influence for several centuries until 1769. There’s no mistaking that the concept focuses heavily on celebrating sense of place, and that’s quite intentional on the owner’s end. During my stay I learned "Ta'aktana" is the indigenous Manggarai word for “green land,” and that journeys to prominent villages to meet with clan chiefs, hear historical tales, and brainstorm collectively during the resort’s conception played into the naming of outlets with words from the traditional dialect, one of many on the island.
The rooms
At minimum, a successful luxury hotel room, to me, is aesthetically pleasing, at least decently sumptuous, well stocked and flawlessly functional. At best, though, it tells stories and incites discovery, and that’s what my ocean-view two-bedroom villa did. There were curiosities, artifacts and antiques abounding, including elaborately woven handicrafts and a gorgeous finely beaded basket baby carrier from the Dayak tribe of Borneo, carefully designed to protect an infant’s soul from malicious spirits, that I’d have purchased if it was an option.
Each of the 70 keys has the same amenities—including Dyson hairdryers and complimentary minibars—as well as aesthetic, which I’ll call refined island elegance with evocative texture, but that plays out differently across room types. My two-bedroom villa had an ocean-view living and dining room that felt wonderfully residential and plush, its vivid macrame wall hanging nodding to the famous pink beach nearby and a soft carpet whose sorbet hues seemed to have been pulled straight from the resort’s sunsets.
I appreciated the generous and lovely refreshment center cabinet (hand-carved with an indigenous weaving pattern) offerings—highlighting local snacks, housemade chocolate chip cookies, Indonesian elixirs and Bintang beer—which is replenished nightly in the villas and once for the suites. Mine also came with the resort’s bottled negroni and martinis. On the other side of a wall of sliding glass was our generous private pool, plus a daybed and several other plump places to sit and enjoy the mosquito-less (at least in September) outdoors.
Down a long, wood-wrapped hallway, the primary bedroom faced the sea, while the second king double room had views of the resort and hills. Both felt luxurious, under vaulted ceilings with carved beams, with petrified wood side tables, brass reading lamps, woven walls and soft leather headboards. The bathrooms featured graceful curved corners, free-standing tubs with bath salts, Sachajuan toiletries in the showers and abundant natural materials.
While I loved the close proximity of my villa to the jetty, ocean and pool, I’d have had plenty of space in one of the 45 entry-level accommodations, which are 743-square-foot junior suites with balconies. The majority look out to the sea, however the ground-floor suites offer views of lush gardens. They, like the villas, have woven lanterns that are lit during turndown service to pay homage to the
lentera
used by the notoriously off-grid Manggarai people, plus woven bamboo boxes inspired by the culture’s ancient storage containers that here hold bathroom amenities.
The resort’s version of a penthouse is the incredibly spacious three-bedroom Mansion, which sleeps six and overlooks the entire resort, like its own standalone estate with a large pool and 24-hour butler service. Perhaps the most eye-catching accommodations are the Overwater Sea Villas, of which there are seven in a direct reference to the seven characteristic houses with conical roofs that sit in the traditional Wae Rebo village nearly 4,000 feet above sea level in the mountains of West Manggarai. Here, the 2,098-square-foot one-bedroom villas with vibrant textiles above the beds also have circular floor plans, but are topped with subtly canted roofs like seashells, and are the decidedly adult-leaning option perfect for honeymoons and other romantic escapes filled with lounging in the sun and under the stars over water. Secluded as they feel, it’s just a short walk to the oversea activity center where kayaks and SUPs are available to paddle between pylons over starfish, sea anemone, clownfish and maybe hawksbill sea turtles.
Food and drink
Breakfast isn’t typically the meal I clamor over, but the spread at gleaming all-day restaurant Leros, with its hammered silver ceiling, mosaic floor and mesmerizing ocean view, brought me extreme joy each morning, as much for the flavors as the personable interactions with chefs, servers and management. Amid a large and decadent a la carte menu of Western and local staples and delicacies, plus an extensive buffet of entirely made-in-house dishes, baked goods and condiments (all with exacting lists of allergens), I most enthusiastically savored the rotating Indonesian options, which ranged from pandan iced coffee,
jamu
and
soto ayam
to
trancam
salad and
bubur sum sum
. The restaurant’s just-spicy-enough chicken
pelalah
spring rolls, a mashup of local flavors with a Vietnamese fave, made such a delicious lunch that I ordered them a second time off the in-villa dining menu. The exacting attention to detail and edible storytelling I found at Leros continued as themes at each dining outlet.
At best, most resorts have one kitchen cooking up the country’s own cuisine, but I noticed the impressive dedication to local at TA’AKTANA translated to multiple venues serving Indonesian fare. More unforgettable food from the archipelago came during a dinner at beachside Umasa, with the golden glow of sunset illuminating our tiffin-like
rantang
-style feast, the highlights of which were
gohu
tuna,
iga babi
pork ribs and syrupy-sweet
kolak pisang
, a sort of fried banana. There are robata-grilled specialties, tableside-sliced nigiri and Japanese fusion dishes on the menu at the al fresco restaurant Taba, where the roof has a spiderweb- or bicycle spoke–like pattern inspired by the distinctive Lodok Lingko rice field, a remnant of thousands of years of Manggarai agricultural tradition. That motif is echoed in the ceramic afternoon tea service platter at Nera Lounge, which held technicolor Indonesian treats (
kue tok
,
mata roda
,
martabak
) to pair with tea that matched the sea, prepared ceremoniously in a glass teapot atop a vibrant marine-inspired mosaic table.
Rounding out the F&B offerings are an overwater lounge called Maiga! with culturally influenced concoctions comprising quirky regional ingredients and live
sasando
music, a coffee hut whose conical shape is taken from the
mbaru niang
houses of Wae Rebo village, and a pool bar serving classic beach bites and an unmissable wagyu
katsu sando
. Not to toot my own horn, but I made mean sopi daiquiri and tamarind paloma cocktails during an ocean-view mixology masterclass celebrating the local spirit made of fermented palm sap distilled in bamboo over fire. Meanwhile, a cook grilled addictive pork sate skewers beside us, a perfect savory accompaniment for the tangy drinks. As we finished, a weekly Destination Discovery cultural showcase was in progress steps away, with a couple local men performing the traditional
caci
fight-dance with ox-skin shields and whips as a show of virility.
The spa
In the local language,
di’a
means “beautiful,” and I very much felt it leaving Di’a Spa, after the sleek Dyson blowout that followed an incredibly soothing, aromatic scalp and hair treatment called Hygge Harmony. It involved a procession of rinses using unique flower- and citrus-infused waters, scalp exfoliation, feel-good combing and head, neck and shoulder massage with herbal poultices and the therapist’s capable hands, too. The four-chair salon-cum–hair spa where I sat for the treatment is part of the more than 17,000-square-foot wellness center, drenched in heavenly light and taking up two floors, with a 24-hour gym, hot and cold plunge pools, and six textured treatment rooms inspired by nearby caves where offerings include regionally inspired rituals, facials and body wraps. In one of the latter, a strong, silken Bajo massage helped some of my chronic tension melt away.
The area
Labuan Bajo has felt like an emerging destination for decades, but this resort is in effect an announcement that the port city, the gateway to Komodo and its dragons, has arrived. When I first visited LBJ, as it’s called, in 2015, it seemed to me a complete secret, a place only a select few traveled through, not
to
, en route to see the endangered species on nearby Rinca or Komodo in Komodo National Park (guilty!), many of them on liveaboard dive boats or chartered phinisi yachts. But in the years since, hospitality companies such as Indonesia’s AYANA opened exciting luxury properties (AYANA Komodo Waecicu Beach is less than a mile to the northwest) that all but compel visitors to spend a little extra time. With an international brand entering the landscape the argument for Labuan Bajo itself as a destination is stronger than ever, especially considering there are now international flights from Singapore and Kuala Lumpur that are bringing it more exposure. During my stay I heard about forthcoming restaurants and resorts being built—as well as saw evidence of the development—that should only add to its appeal as a quieter alternative to Bali with just as much fascination to capture travelers’ attention. Coming from busy Bali, I felt the blink-and-you-missed-it short airport transfer was a dream, but my three-night stay far too short for all there is to do in the immediate vicinity.
The service
I found the service at TA’AKTANA to be at the level of a Four Seasons or Rosewood type of property, which I thought said a lot considering it was just three months old at the time of my visit and in a fairly remote location. Some 99 percent of the wonderfully professional staff is from Indonesia, with nearly half from Flores, and they were incredibly helpful and solicitous, going out of their way to explain local dishes at breakfast, describe the region they call home, entertain my daughter, and tailor our overland tour to my interests and particular needs of traveling with a baby.
For families
From the moment we arrived, the staff showered my baby with extra affection and attention, making it crystal clear this was a very family-friendly resort. There are two family villas in which the second bedroom is tyke-oriented with cute sea-themed wallpaper and a lofted play area, plus a highly interactive kids club, shallow kids’ pool, and all the necessary equipment and gear little ones require at mealtimes, bedtime and beyond. A cushy teepee occupied by an ever-rotating cast of sweet towel animals and flowers for my little one was a nice touch, too. Additionally, TA’AKTANA has an Olympic-sized 50-meter pool where swimmers don goggles and caps for laps plus a sprawling beachside pool for all ages, with a swim-up bar and plenty of loungers. The resort’s Little Sea Nomads program provides children with ample hands-on opportunities to learn about the indigenous Bajau people (sea nomads) through projects such as carving and decorating mini wooden long boats as well as educational guided outings to kayak and snorkel.
Eco effort
From the outset, TA’AKTANA has modeled good behavior when it comes to protecting the nature that makes it so very unique. They’re working toward a zero-waste goal in the food and beverage department and, eventually, the entire resort. So far efforts include cassava drinking straws, their own water bottling plant using only glass, and zero-plastic amenities. The staff perform regular Flores Sea clean-ups by boat, and they collaborate on Bersih Bajo (Clean Bajo) events. There are future plans for guest-facing coral reef planting and conservation activities. Local sourcing is another aspect of TA’AKTANA’s eco approach, and that includes partnerships with nearby farmers, artisans and business owners for restaurants and souvenir gifts for guests. The coffee hut roasts Indonesian beans in-house and the pool bar serves an “upcycled” espresso martini using its leftover grinds. Hair scrunchies, coasters and table napkins are made from repurposed cotton batik fabrics.
Accessibility
There is one first-floor accessible junior suite, and the resort’s elevators are wheelchair friendly with Braille buttons. All guests may use golf cart service to get around the hilly property, and most public and communal spaces, such as the library full of interesting relics and antiques and overwater bar, are accessible via wraparound ramps.
Anything left to mention?
Seeing as it’s in the gateway to Komodo National Park, taking a full-day boat trip from the resort to witness the Jurassic-seeming creatures in their natural habitat is a worthy pursuit, especially considering it includes jaunts to the mesmerizing pink beach and Padar Island for an uphill hike with a quintessential view as the reward. I’ve personally visited the adequately creepy dragons twice, once by slow local boat and another time on a luxury phinisi, but after experiencing firsthand TA’AKTANA’s conscious handling of cultural outings, I’d expect the resort’s tour to be excellent.
