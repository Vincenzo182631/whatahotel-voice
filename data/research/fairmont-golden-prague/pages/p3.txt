Why book Fairmont Golden Prague?
You’ve seen
The Brutalist
—now stay in the hotel. Thanks to the Adrien Brody film, brutalist architecture has been having a moment in 2025, which makes the opening of Fairmont Golden Prague, set in a brutalist classic from 1974, very well-timed. But you should also go for the unbeatable views of the River Vltava and, on the other side, the city’s glorious historic skyline at sunset. Oh, and the views from the outdoor pool. And the top-floor bar. And some of the most creative Czech cuisine in
Prague
. Safe to say the Canadian hotel group has ramped up the city scene with this audacious new opening.
Set the scene
Does Prague have a riviera? Well, it does now. Set just back from the banks of the Vltava, the Fairmont Golden Prague is reviving a whole stretch of the riverfront, with fountain-plashed landscaped gardens, a new art gallery and a terrace bar and café that will come into its own during the summer. Rising above it all is the geometric magnificence of Seventies modernism realized in concrete and golden tiles—all texture and shadow and low-slung, pavilion-style spaces flooded with light, refracting through original abstract stained-glass windows and globular chandeliers.
The backstory
Pity the poor brutalist building in Prague. The world and her uncle come to the Czech capital to watch the 15th-century astronomical clock whirr and strike the hour, take endless selfies by the statues on Charles Bridge, and quaff beer in cobblestoned medieval courtyards. Does anyone spare a thought for a flat-faced concrete slab built in 1974? As it happens, they do. Originally designed by Czech architect Karel Filsak, it opened as the InterContinental Prague, it was a glamorous Western arrival in the drab era of Seventies Communism, designed for “Come Fly Me” PanAm passengers and visiting dignitaries and celebrities—Ray Charles, Richard Nixon, and Elton John; even Carlos the Jackal. There was a hard-currency shop in the lobby where you could buy jeans and cigarettes for those with the right money. A different era: the hotel is now owned by three Czech entrepreneurs who have spent a fortune remodeling it—all the original crumbling concrete has been replaced and façade tiles replaced—and are using the hotel to showcase their country’s design history, from glassmaking and art to the classic film posters of the 1960s and 70s. The lobby now has a bar named in honour of Czech-born
One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest
director Milos Forman, complete with pill-shaped lights, while the city’s various symbols have been cast in ceramics and decorate a feature wall.
The rooms
I’m a big fan of a window seat, and from the one in my suite here, I could look out over a scene plucked from Richard Scarry’s
Busy, Busy Town
—red trams trundling, riverboats cruising, bicycles spinning. Echoing the Fairmont Golden Prague’s brutalist architecture, bedrooms use color sparingly but draw deep on texture and pattern, with geometric fabrics on cushions and carpet, and bathrooms carved from warm stone—a white tub taking pride of place. The hotel owners have made sure Czech glassware is used throughout, appearing here as amber-colored bedside lights, a clutch of lovely drinks glasses, and the centerpiece: a beautiful glass sliding door into the bathroom, textured to resemble rainwater. Thankfully, there was nothing brutalist about the bed, which takes an altogether softer, more comforting approach.
Food and drink
Czech food may once have been all about the goulash, schnitzel, and the pork knuckle—hearty winter food with only the occasional cameo role for a salad leaf—but Prague’s chefs have been reinventing their cuisine of late, rummaging around the country’s diverse larder and bringing more vegetables into play. First, though, a spoiler alert: the fine-dining restaurant, Zlata Praha, is set on the top floor and has unimpeachable views over the spires and towers of the Old Town—as you approach, the door slides open for the big reveal. Make sure you get here for the golden hour before sunset. With this sort of view they could serve Coco Pops and I’d be happy, but chef Maros Jambor (formerly of Sketch and Eleven Madison Park) has ambition, conjuring playful dishes that surprise and entertain while staying true to Central European ingredients. A starter of ancient wheat, lion’s mane, and truffle was something completely different—all umami crunch, not unlike an Indian bel poori‚while a pea custard with sugar snap was a bowl of fresh chlorophyll; rabbit ballotine a perfect little example of French technique. Desserts include a caramel popcorn that you break with your spoon for the tarte-tatin-like Jerusalem artichoke beneath.
The sommelier will guide you through the wine list, which veers towards Old World labels but also showcases some Czech wines (the country’s vintages are getting better and better), though for cocktails head next door to the river-facing bar. Order the Bonsai 405 and a little tree is carried over nestling a glass of Becherovka, a herby local digestif, mixed with Thai basil and Champagne. From the menu you can graze on sushi and minced pork banh mi sliders and kimchi fries.
The first-floor Greenhouse, meanwhile, coaxes locals in with an all-day menu of beer-hall favorites such as spiced pork crackling, roasted bone marrow, and mushroom goulash, along with meat cuts and pike perch hot off the Josper grill. It’s a chance to try Czech classics such as Kuladja soup (poached egg and grilled potato) and vetrnek—a caramel-y pastry that was hugely popular during the Communist period. Breakfast is served in the Kafka Brasserie—somewhat confusingly named after Czech artist Cestmire Kakfa rather than the writer, whose wooden sculptures decorate the ceiling. I ordered a boiled egg and it arrived in a square-cut brutalist egg cup. Bravo.
The service
Bowler-hatted doormen usher you inside, where cheery, dapper-suited staff await behind a brace of mirror-tiled desks. As you’d expect from a team overseen by the Fairmont group, the service is pin-sharp (after realizing I’d come without my swimming trunks, a pair was swiftly dispatched to my room) with staff made up of locals and an international crew, some of whom had learned their craft from the
Fairmont Dubai
.
The neighborhood
Slap bang on Prague’s Bond Street in the Old Town—if Bond Street had a whopping great river flowing at one end. The art nouveau buildings of Parizska Street flutter with labels including Prada and Hermès, although more atmospheric is the historic Jewish Quarter that borders it, dating back to the 12th century. From the hotel it’s only a short walk down to the Old Town Square, with its astronomical clock, the twin-spired Gothic cathedral that resembles a medieval spaceship, and rather too many shops selling chimney cakes to tourists—nice to have some distance from the crowds. In the other direction is the Vltava River, which you can walk along or cross over to reach Prague Castle, the Kunsthalle—a contemporary art gallery recently opened in a former electricity station—and the green space of Letna Park. Most of the city sights are within easy walking distance—I’d recommend the Museum of Decorative Arts for interiors as beautiful as a Fabergé egg. And for anyone remotely interested in architecture, I’d recommend going on a walking safari of the city’s modernist landmarks. Thanks to my guide Vlasta, arranged by the Fairmont concierge, I saw parts of Prague I’d never glimpsed before, from the Bauhaus-inspired Trade Fair Palace to the socialist-realist
Jalta Hotel
.
Who comes here?
Early days, but during my visit many locals were keen to see the transformation, booking tables in the restaurant and bar for sunset views. Being a Fairmont, it will naturally draw in an American clientele—Prague is a firm fixture on the European Grand Tour—and will certainly give the nearby Four Seasons and Mandarin Oriental a run for their money.
The spa
Prague has some top-ranking hotel wellness spaces (the Mandarin’s, in a Renaissance-era chapel, is particularly impressive), but the one at the Fairmont is the sunniest and has the only indoor-outdoor pool in town—an L-shaped pool surrounded by top-to-bottom wooden paneling, with splooshy hydrotherapy jets and sliding doors that you can glide through to the riverside garden terrace. With sun loungers all around, it has views to the hotel’s art gallery (opening in
June
) and the Chechuv Bridge, as well as curious passersby on the river walkway above. Back inside, there are three saunas and a steam room, as well as treatment rooms for massages and facials—including the signature Czech Crystal Beauty facial, an intricate 90-minute treatment that uses crystals to soothe away stress. A well-kitted-out Technogym faces out over the garden terrace.
Eco effort
As part of the project to revitalize the area, Fairmont has been greenscaping the riverfront in front of the hotel, and has plans to create a green corridor along Pařížská Street. It uses heat pump tech for heating and cooling, rainwater collection for garden irrigation, and recycles its food waste, among other measures; single-use plastics are banned, and all the eggs on the menu are free range.
Accessibility
The hotel has 16 accessible rooms with wider entrance doors, fitted with lower light switches and beds, spacious bathrooms, and grab bars in the bathrooms.
Anything else to mention?
Try not to be confused by some of the names. The Kafka Brasserie, where breakfast is served, isn’t named after the author of
Metamorphosis
but Czech artist Cestmire Kafka, whose angular wooden sculptures adorn the ceiling. Likewise, Goldeneye Bar isn’t named after the Bond film at all, but the Chinese duck.
