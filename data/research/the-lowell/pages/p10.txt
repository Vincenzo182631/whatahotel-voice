Flawless boutique option on the Upper East Side

Reviewed by
Rebecca Misner
Why book?
Most amazing trips to New York–or rather any trip to New York–involves some level of sensory overload. The sights, the smells, the tastes, the pace–it’s all so exhilarating and also so very exhausting. The Lowell is a soothing balm, a cosseting antidote to all that stimulation. Checking into this Upper East Side stunner is a portal to a different era, a time when civility, impeccable hospitality, and understated elegance ruled the day.
Set the scene
Located on a leafy block of 63rd Street, just east of Madison Avenue and a block and change from Central Park, the Lowell has the feel of a luxe, residential apartment, and the welcoming staff make you feel, upon arrival, as if you are truly coming home. Even though it neighbors some of the city’s toniest boutiques–Hermés, Goyard, Fred Leighton–and some of the loveliest residential homes in the Upper East Side, the Lowell, while thoroughly elegant, does not feel pretentious. The lobby, just off the check-in area, is a small, cozy space with a wood burning fireplace and fresh bouquets of flowers. Next to it is the Club Room, a sophisticated, beautifully decorated space with its own bar (and another fireplace), which acts as a sort of extended lobby for hotel guests and their guests during the day. You’ll see men and women in office attire having a drink but also parents and their kids reading or hanging out. It feels very intimate–a lovely private-public hybrid where guests can mingle if they like or grab one of the oversized art books and just relax in a lovely room for a beat. It’s that unicorn of a space in New York City for when you want to get away from the bustle but you’re not ready to go up to your room.
The backstory
The Lowell was built in the 1920 as a luxury apartment house (Dorothy Parker lived there for a spell after she left the Algonquin; F. Scott and Zelda spent time there as well). It’s currently privately owned by the Chartouni family (one of the few remaining family-owned hotels left in New York City) and while this is perhaps a vague thing to say, there’s a palpable sense that people–rather than a corporate entity–who care deeply about the hotel and not just the bottom line, are behind it. There’s a warmth and an attention to detail that feels personal.
The rooms
There are 74 rooms and suites ranging from 400-square-foot one bedrooms (this is quite big by NYC standards) to the three-bedroom, 3000-square-foot penthouse suite. Some have woodburning fireplaces, outdoor terraces, and kitchens–just right for when you really want to live out that pied-à-terre fantasy. All of the guest spaces were updated by Michael S. Smith, the designer behind the White House refresh during Obama’s presidency, and are understated, elegant and supremely comfortable (cashmere throws, Frette sheets, proper closets and spacious bathrooms). I stayed in one of the one-bedroom suites, which was decorated in soothing neutrals (grays, taupes, creams) and had a long all-marble bathroom with a big glassed in shower (with excellent pressure–always a gamble in NYC hotels) and a deep bathtub, as well as a spacious separate living area with a sofa and chairs, a little table, and a proper desk with a large monitor that you can connect your laptop to. My favorite touch was a separate Deco-era vanity table and tufted white chair outside of the bathroom–a serious perk when you’re traveling with someone and you’re both trying to get ready at the same time. The entire vibe of my room was quiet, calm and cosetting–the decor was tasteful and the art lovely (framed drawings of Ancient Greek statues). While some hotels hit you over the head with the latest design tropes, the rooms here are timeless.
Food and drink
There are four different dining and drinking venues. Majorelle is the hotel’s main restaurant; it's open to the public and most days serves lunch, dinner and a lovely afternoon tea. The menu is French with a sprinkling of Moroccan in honor of its namesake, the French artist Jacques Majorelle who moved to Morocco in 1917 and created the stunning Majorelle garden in Marrakech. In keeping with the hotel, the space–including the outside area–is beautifully designed and the service is spot on. (The Dover sole was perfection and the dark chocolate mousse a must.) Adjacent to the restaurant is Jacques Bar, a clubby, jewel-box of a space with lots of honey-hued oak paneling, velvet drapes and a gold-checked wall treatment. There’s a handful of stools at the actual bar and the rest of the space is taken up mostly with round, two-top tables with leather club chairs. It’s a lovely, intimate space just right for drinking a martini and having a light dinner. It was quiet on Wednesday night when I arrived but picked up steam as the weekend approached and Saturday night felt very buzzy. The Pembroke Room, located on the second floor, is the guest-only breakfast area–it does a nice job with the usual suspects (eggs every way, yogurt and granola, pancakes, etc.); on the weekends, it offers a separate brunch menu. The Club Room, as mentioned, is guests-only until 5 pm and then it’s open to the public; it has a separate menu with a wide variety of alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages. Guests can also order off the Jacques Bar food menu and eat in the Club Room.
The spa
There is no spa but there is a very good small fitness center that has everything you might need, including the latest cardio equipment and weights (plus free healthy snacks!)
The neighborhood/area
As noted, the Lowell is in the heart of the Upper East Side. It’s flanked by Central Park to the west and is a short (like one-minute) walk to just about every luxury boutique you might want, including a number of originally downtown brands (Ulla Johnson, Khaite, Foundrae, Toteme) that have opened outposts on Madison Ave. The hotel is a few blocks from the marvelous newly renovated Frick Collection as well as an easy walk to other major museums, including the Guggenheim, the Met, and the Cooper Hewitt. The subway is still the best way to get around the city and the Lowell is an easy walk to several different stations and subway lines.
The service
It is impeccable–the rare mix of efficient, professional, genuine, and warm. There was not a single service hiccup my entire stay, from arrival to departure. The doormen recognize you and welcome you back. The front desk staff know your name (you don’t need to give them your room when you come back from being out–they just hand you your tasseled key fob and ask if you need anything). The housekeeper who was in charge of the floor my room was on would greet me in the morning when I left for the day–asking if everything was ok, if I had enough glass bottles of water the night before or if she should leave more tonight. And my room was meticulously cleaned daily and then tidied and readied for the night. Every aspect of service felt considered and intentional and not at all performative.
For families
The Lowell is very welcoming of children–both human children and those of the pet variety–and has a number of large, multi-bedroom suites with kitchens and plenty of living space that make traveling as a family a lot more comfortable.
Eco effort
Bottled water comes in glass, not plastic, bottles; bathroom products come in metal tubes, not single use plas
t
ic containers; you have the option to reuse your towels and to not have your bed linens changed daily.
Accessibility
ADA-compliant rooms are available.
