Aman Kyoto
A retreat that brings guests to nature as much as to the traditions of central Kyoto.

Reviewed by
Danielle Demetriou
Why book?
For a a modern riff on Japan’s soul-soothing ryokan inns—and a moment of mindful escapism in a “secret garden” filled with moss-covered pathways, towering trees and minimalist timber pavillions in a quiet Kyoto hideaway that feels a million miles from the downtown tourist crowds.
Set the scene
Crossing the threshold of Aman
Kyoto
is a bit like straying into a fairytale. Picture wide moss-wrapped stone pathways flanked by towering trees, walls of huge cut boulders overgrown with plants, forested pathways leading to temple-like staircases. The heart of Aman Kyoto is this so-called “secret garden”which treads a delicate line between wild and cultivated. Scattered discreetly among the trees are the clean-cut black timber silhouettes of a half dozen minimalist pavilions, housing 24 guest rooms and a duo of two-bedroom villas, as well as an intimately deluxe spa and two restaurants. Nature—and its subtlest micro-shifts—are the main protagonists, from the green humidity of summer, the sound of cicadas ringing thick in the air to the fiery blaze of autumn leaves (courtesy of approximately 3,000 Japanese maple trees) and the fresh cloud-like bursts of springtime sakura. Its location is key to its serene and peaceful DNA—it’s hidden in the forested foothills of a mountain on the northeastern fringes of the ancient capital. It’s not going to be the perfect setting for those who prefer all the sites right on their doorstep—but the plus side is that it is blissfully peaceful compared to the tourist packed city hotspots. It’s also within walking distance of legendary Kinkakuji (Golden Pavilion) and countless other historic sites are easily accessible by car. Or for those who want a taste of a quieter more local Kyoto, electric biycles are available for exploring the neighbourhood’s tranquil lanes, lined with traditional food makers, temples and shrines.
The backstory
According to much-whispered Aman legend, the garden was initially created by one of Japan’s most famous textile collectors who had hoped to buid a museum to house his collection of
obi
(decorative kimono sashes). His plans came to an end when he passed away and the land passed into the hands of Aman. Two decades of navigating strict Kyoto building regulations followed—before the hotel finally opened in November 2019. Today, the hotel happily defies headlines about Kyoto’s overtourism issues. The grounds are blissfully quiet – aside from its immersive soundtrack of birdsong, insects, forest breezes—with enough green space to never feel busy. Yet for all its natural wildness, the experience is sharpened into the highest expression of contemporary luxury through carefully composed Aman ingredients—the perfectly-measured minimalist design of the pavilions by Kerry Hill Architects, the intuitively warm hospitality of the friendly and professional staff and a layered echoing of the micro-season of the moment through the top quality cuisine, the artful ikebana flowers, the spa treatments. As longtime collaborator Kerry Hill’s last project before he passed away, it’s also a place that clearly has a special place in the heart of the Aman family—as reflected in a deeply peaceful jewel-green moss garden dedicated to the late designer.
The rooms
Housed in several different pavilions scattered along garden paths, rooms reflect a smoothly-executed modern riff on Japanese ryokan inns. Spacious, serene and perfectly poised between traditional Japanese aesthetics and smooth modern luxury, the rooms have a warm crafted palette, a minimalist simplicity and an intangible sense of stillness. Alongside cinematic floor to ceiling views framing the mindful minutiae of forest life, there are aromatic tatami mat floors, organic
tsuchikabe
earthen walls, expanses of light wood screens (one hiding a high-tech TV), bespoke Hill-designed curved back chairs and even a
tokonoma
alcove, more typically found in a tearoom than a hotel room. Frequently bathed in a tree-filtering play of light and shadows, the minimalist alcove is home to the unglazed raw textures of a vase by ceramicist Teppei Terada containing a hint of the microseason (a plucked forest flower or angular foliage), alongside a hanging scroll with ink abstractions mirroring the landscape by Kyoto artist Yuji Sakai. The layout is comfortable and spacious—with sliding wood screens opening above the bed, connecting bathroom and living space and deepening the spatial experience further. A key highlight? The vast hinoki bathtub—big enough to fit an entire family (forward planning is required, however: it’s so big, it takes maybe 30 minutes to fill).
Food and drink
While it’s quite tempting to disappear into the solitary cosmos of your serene guest room and never remerge again, the hotel’s social heartbeat is The Living Pavilion—an elegantly crafted contemporary restaurant where breakfast, all-day dining, afternoon teas and dinners unfold. Inspired by the concept “land to table”, the menu blends Kyoto home-cooking style cuisine with Western comfort food, using a raft of local ingredients (miso and soy sauce are sourced from centuries-old Matsuno Shoyu, a short walk from the hotel). Dinner is an exquisitely-presented highlight, with a recent summer menu ranging from a cooling corn soup with sea urchin, ginger jelly and bacon foam to tender roasted venison loin with a type of fermented soy bean
natto
that originates at local Daitokuji temple. Breakfast is relaxed and tasty, with both Western and Japanese options, again using a medley of local ingrdients, including Kyoto free range eggs (nab an outdoor table overlooking the gardens). Japanese food devotees should make a pilgrimage to the hotel’s second restaurant Taka-an, which offers a expertly executed modern
kaiseki
experience at a long wooden counter. Dishes are subtly crafted and imbued with seasonal local flavours (a personal favourite? The quietly layered delicacy of a steaming conger eel soup with a hint of yuzu). The sharply curated tea pairing is also recommended.
The spa
Restorative slowing down is centre stage at Aman Kyoto—with even a short stroll through the grounds feeling like an immersive dose of forest bathing. A sense of the sacred imbues the atmosphere at the spa, housed in a single-storey pavilion in the forest. Intimate and time-haltingly calm, it’s home to three treatment suites and onsen hot spring baths, separated by gender—both indoors and outside, with bounders scattered in the mineral-rich waters. In true Aman style, treatments are meticulously researched and perfectly delivered. Rooted deeply in Kyoto culture, my spa journey begins with a sip of seasonal black bean tea and a foot scrub using a mix of green tea, salt, sake lees (from 300-year-old Matsui Sake Brewery—purveyor of sake to the gods at nearby Kinkakuji Temple) and an indulgent sprinkling of gold, before a restorative massage with an aromatic mint and cedar oil.
The neighborhood/area
It feels refreshingly far removed from the downtown tourists crowds. The hotel’s bicycles are perfect for quiet explorations of a more local side of Kyoto’s city DNA—from local temple complexes and shrines (many without tourists) to centuries-old family producers and artisans, lining low-key streets. Iconic UNESCO World Heritage Site the Golden Pavilion (Kinkakuji) is also within walking distance, while many other famous landmarks and curated Aman experiences can be accessed by car.
The service
Staff are open-minded and friendly—qualities underpinned by a quiet mastery of Japan’s famed
omotenashi
hospitality. Refreshingly, it feels less formal than some other five-star hotels in Japan, with many staff engaging in friendly and open conversations. It also feels thoughtful and personal—with staff writing handwritten notes to follow up on questions relating to names of artists or seasonal plants. A string of curated and bespoke experiences are also available for guests—from ikebana flower arranging and private Zen meditation sessions in the garden to explorations of tea plantations in tea hub Uji.
For families
Despite the grown-up edge to the serene atmosphere and decor, it’s peaceful for visitors of all ages. Children will love the gardens as well as activities (from origami to acorn and leaf hunting art sessions) while kids menus are also available.
Eco effort
A number of sustainability efforts underpin Aman Kyoto—including initiatives to protect ailing fireflies, known as
hotaru
(helping local communities keep clean a nearby stream); donating money to a local charity dedicated to keeping waterways clean when guests choose not to change bedding or towels; plus collaborating with local farmers and sourcing more than a third of its fresh vegetables from surronding areas.
Accessibility
Despite the uneven stone paths and wild forest walkways, several rooms close to the entrance are specially designed for wider accessibility, with buggies also on standby to transport guests on the daily garden tours if needed.
Anything left to mention?
It’s perhaps the perfect place to end a Kyoto trip—there are few better ways to recover from an intense does of temple fatigue than with a curated dose of forest bathing and onsen bathing in the deluxe serenity of Aman Kyoto.
