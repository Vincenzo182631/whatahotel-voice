Anantara Convento di Amalfi Grand Hotel
A stay at this five-star hotel perched on a cliff’s edge on the Amalfi Coast may feel like a religious experience—and with very good reason.

Reviewed by

Why book Anantara Convento di Amalfi Grand Hotel?
To dip into the life-affirming, lemons-and-sunshine lifestyle of the Costiera Amalfitana – a holiday spot since the Romans – from the cosseted surrounds of the second European opening from the ever-expanding Anantara group.
Set the scene
From this cliffside-carved eyrie 80 metres above the sea, the whole of picaresque, rollicking
Amalfi
and the glistening waters of the Gulf of Salerno are laid out in panorama.
But up here on the terraces of the Anantara Grand Hotel Convento di Amalfi, the shrieks of day-tripping cruise passengers, zipping back and forth in Tango-orange tenders, are pleasantly faint. All is peaceful under the long, lazy loggia laden with vibrant fuschia bougainvillaea – the Passeggiata dei Monaci – and in the neat orchards low-hanging with fat, bobbly
Amalfi
lemons.
While the spirit of this hotel speaks more to the pleasure-seeking days of the Belle Époque (when the building first became a hotel), the shell it occupies has a certain austere yet spiritually powerful identity that cannot be eradicated.
Tucked behind the historic 13th-century monastery-hermitage, like a secret, is part of the old Arab-Norman cloister, collonaded with interlaced arches, the sacristy and the sweet Chapel of San Francesco, a single-nave, rib-vaulted church with Baroque paintings and mouldings.
The new Anantara chapter has left the original sandy-yellow colour of the facade of the convent unchanged, and the current interior design, whilst modern and full of creature comforts, espouses a simplicity that seems to reference the building's origins. Local woods, leathers, natural fibres, and precious metals bring a softness that is blended with the contemporary accents Anantara is known for. But there is a certain ascetic simplicity to this labyrinthine heritage building that remains relatively unadorned.
The backstory
It’s complicated. There has been a religious presence in this cliffside nook since 1212. Originally Cistercian, Il Convento dei Cappuccini was established by Cardinal Pietro Capuano. In 1583, the Capuchin friars of the Franciscan order took over. They were forced out various times over the following centuries – by the anti-clerical Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, in the 18th century; by the church-hating Napoleon in the early 19th century (returning some time after the 1815 restoration of the European monarchies); and after
Italy
’s 1861 unification, which sounded the death knell of the Papal States.
The town of Amalfi took over in 1866 and made the Convento a nautical academy. Then, in 1882, by which time the culture of the European Grand Tour amongst wealthy British and North American youth of rank had peaked, the convent finally transmogrified into a hotel (like other convents along this fabled coast).
The NH hotel group had been in charge since a 2010 renovation then, in spring of this year, Minor Hotels unveiled the Anantara Grand Hotel Convento di Amalfi, following restoration and renovation works that were packed into a six-month window (according to the local-council diktats).
The rooms
The 52 rooms and suites, scattered across multiple levels in a labyrinthine, anthill-like structure, come in a multitude of shapes and sizes. Many were first built as monks’ quarters, and original tiles, vaulted ceilings and lime-washed walls have been retained and elevated with rich soft furnishings, sculptural furniture and the high-tech comforts of an Anantara. The Suite Del Priore – named for its setting in the old priory – has original frescoes and cloister views; the Suite Dell’Eremita has its own lemon grove. My room overlooked the cloisters and chapel to the rear and the Gulf of Salerno to the front (from its own hot-tub terrace). Even the more modest rooms enjoy the same uplifting Mediterranean vistas.
Food and drink
Dining assiduously zones in on all things “Costiera”, from hyper-local suppliers of ingredients including wines. Dei Cappucinni chef Claudio Lanuto utilises seasonal ingredients grown in the “convent garden” for the a-la-carte dishes, sensory tasting menus, private dining (which can be arranged in the gardens), and cooking classes. Gino Sorbillo, the
Naples
pizza maestro with a national presence, has created La Locanda della Canonica Pizzeria, which presents eight pizzas to create a journey through Campania, using anchovies from Cetara, provolone Monaco and mozzarella and ricotta from Agerola. Sorbillo, pronouncing that there’s “nothing more intimate than feeding another,” tells us over pizza fritta (with a frenzy of toppings, from mortadella and fried basil to confit tomato, stracciatella and capers), that he would still choose his grandfather's classic pizza as his last meal. “Pomodoro, garlic e oregano. Semplice.”
The spa
A limestone-clad sea-view space with three treatment rooms is yet to open. Meanwhile, the signature Convento Di Amalfi massage with citrus oils is offered al-fresco from a top-level terrace cabana. A Technogym-equipped fitness space lurks amid the olive groves and a cliffside
swimming pool
awaits at the end of the loggia.
The neighbourhood
Amalfi, along with Positano, is one of the charming tourist towns of this honey-pot coast, but it’s awash with visitors, so it’s pleasant to float 80 metres above sea level in this hallowed and peaceful spot and then pop down for a raucous supper in town when the daytrippers have departed. Amalfi is on the precipice of the Sentiero Degli Dei, the “Path of the Gods” hike between Praiano and Positano, as well as the Path of the Lemons from Minori to Maiori. Boat trips to
Capri
and Positano are on the doorstep, as are hair-raising Vespa tours on hairpin roads and private visits to Pompei and Herculaneum. The hotel works with a local Franciscan friar from Ravello, Friar Marcus, to introduce interested guests to the chapel, or whisk them on tours of Ravello, his local parish (and the home of Villas Cimbrone and Rufolo).
Who comes here?
Americans and a scattering of Europeans in search of the fabled Dolce Vita of this coast, and the trend of Asian and Middle East guests who are fans of the brand.
The service
Warm, Italian and hyper-local, with a seemingly strong contingent of pre-Anantara staffers, which gives a sense of continuity and harmony. Special props to Roberto who saves guests’ lives every time they cross the road in front of the hotel by body-blocking Vespa traffic.
Eco effort
Sustainable projects include locally-sourced produce and a single-use plastic ban, plus Anantara runs Dollars for Deeds, where guests can donate $1 per night, matched by them, donated to local causes (such as the restoration of play areas in Ravello).
Accessibility
The higgledy-piggledy nature of the building, with any number of steps going in multiple different directions, does not initially scream accessibility, but there are three accessible rooms and lifts accessing every level.
Is it worth it?
Yes. The monastery-hermitage that the Anantara Grand Hotel Convento di Amalfi lives in has peered over this endlessly entrancing Tyrrhenian view of sunsets and moonshine since the 13th century, which is priceless.
