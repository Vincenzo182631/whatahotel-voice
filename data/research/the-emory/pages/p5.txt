The Emory
Steel, glass and a whole new sort of Hyde Park Corner stay.

Reviewed by
Toby Skinner
Why book?
Because
London
’s first all-suite hotel is a bit different: unashamedly modern, discreet, fabulously expensive, and filled with Damien Hirst pieces, with a spa for an elite that plans to live forever.
Set the scene
Looking across from
Hyde Park
, The Emory is a glassy box with protruding steel outriggers, somewhere between nautical and industrial. No red brick or Portland stone here, among so many other stalwart London hotels. The entrance to this one, down the Old Barracks Yard side street, is barely marked. Reception is just a little glass box, which most guests will arrive at in a virtually silent electric BMW i7. They’ll meet their Emory Assistant, essentially a concierge/PA for the duration of their stay, before encountering two figures in an open-air courtyard just beyond reception: a feminine humanoid insect and a skinless man with a pair of scissors, like Gunther Von Hagens specimens gone rogue—both the creations of Damien Hirst, whose butterflies and floral AI paintings are dotted throughout the property. Upstairs, the suites—none smaller than 6,400 square feet—are soft-edged and soft-toned, many with privileged views over Hyde Park. Few London hotels feel quite as honed in on the post-
Succession
vogue for quiet luxury, or as primed for the global billionaire class.
The backstory
This is the latest offering from Maybourne, behind
Claridge’s
,
The Connaught
,
The Berkeley
next door and various global properties, including the
Côte d’Azur
to Beverly Hills. It’s remarkable for being London’s first all-suite hotel, and a departure of sorts for a group known mostly for heritage grandiosity. But The Emory is most notable as one of the last projects of the late Pompidou architect Richard Rogers, who came up with the plan almost two decades ago with former Maybourne head Paddy McKillen. The hotel is semi-suspended on steel sails and sitting on anti-vibration bearings to offset the rumbles of the Piccadilly Line below. Rogers is among several big names associated with The Emory—alongside Damien Hirst, the chef Jean-Georges Vongerichten, and the celebrity fitness guru Tracy Anderson, who also has a studio in the hotel’s longevity-focused Surrenne spa.
Six renowned interior designers were involved, with public spaces by super-yacht designer Rémi Tessier, who is also behind the Claridge’s glass penthouse. Four designers were given two floors each—André Fu (Claridge’s Spa), Pierre Yves Rochon (
The Savoy
), Alexandra Champalimaud (
Raffles Singapore
), and Patricia Urquiola (
Six Senses Rome
)—while the 3,200-square-foot penthouse is by London-based Rigby & Rigby. The Emory isn’t messing about, but it’s not shouty either. The word everyone uses is “discretion,” with guests able to rent out whole floors—something Louis Vuitton has already done for its top brass.
The rooms
The various designers may bring their signature traits—Fu’s Asian-influenced Zen geometry, Urquiola’s bulbous Milanese modernism–but there is a cohesion to the 60 suites spread across nine floors. Tactile woods and marble abound, and Tessier's fondness for curves over straight lines is reflected in all the soft-colored spaces. I stayed in the 2,150-square-foot Emory Park Suite, designed by Fu, its great expanses of glass overlooking Hyde Park in the way that the
The Mark
or
The Ritz
in
New York
peer over
Central Park
. For all the impressive sprawl, it’s very much an understated space filled with minutely detailed luxury: the whirr of the black Grind x Emory coffee or the Toto toilet; the smooth glide of every hidden drawer, or the lift of the leather cases for the Dyson products in the dressing room that’s as big as some hotel rooms. Even the bamboo toothbrushes in the dental kit come with the sharply graphic Emory logo. The bar isn’t a minibar, but a marble-fluted glass cabinet stocked with The Lakes gin, Konik’s Tail vodka, or Michter’s small batch bourbon, all included in the price of a stay. As well as tasty snacks, one drawer is filled with brand new Apple chargers, in case the leather-topped wireless charger by the bed doesn’t work. A QR code gives access to a library of newspapers, and the widescreen TVs broadcast the key channels, including Sky Sports. With the Emory Assistant on call at all times, there’s a prevailing sense that these suites are set up for longer stays.
Food and drink
Overlooking Knightsbridge and already buzzing with what feels like a post-work Chelsea crowd, abc kitchens is an offshoot of Jean-Georges Vongerichten’s three ABC restaurants in New York. Despite the very Richard Rogers steel beams that dissect the place, there’s a lightness to it: Tessier's wavy wooden banquettes face floor-to-ceiling windows looking out onto Knightsbridge; and hanging on the walls are floral Damien Hirsts painted over AI-generated images. The decor matches the organic dishes listed in menu sections such as “Light and bright”. Produced from a gleaming open kitchen by chef Benjamin Boeynaems, they feature British ingredients but all have been on New York menus—from a subtle, flower-topped Dorset crab toast to an Orkney sea scallop tartare with shiso and kohlrabi, and—Obama’s favorite—the spring pea guacamole with sunflower seeds and crunchy tortillas. There are cheeseburgers, black truffle pizzas, and pork confit tacos, but the heroes are dishes like south coast John Dory in a fragrant herby broth, or seasonal white asparagus with artichoke hollandaise. On the top floor, and open to guests only, the vibe is different (and quieter during soft-opening)—more Jetsons meets
Mad Men
, with circular mid-century leather swivel chairs, a rotating cigar cabinet and epic, panoramic views of London.
The spa
Over four gorgeously marbled subterranean floors, and with an AI soundtrack that never repeats itself, it’s the longevity-focused Surrenne holistic spa that may turn out to be The Emory’s biggest talking point. Open to non-guest members for $12,500 a year, it’s not just its hardware that impresses—the gorgeous Rémi Tessier pool with blue bubble doors, the submarine-like hyperbaric chamber, the yoga room with a go-anywhere screen, and the spring-floored studio for workouts by US celebrity fitness guru Tracy Anderson—but also the best-in-class experts behind so much of it. All members start with full medical checkups, and the doctors here are overseen by an advisory board that includes the likes of the renowned epigeneticist David Sinclair and the mindfulness-oriented clinical psychologist Shauna Shapiro. Guests and members have access to nutrition programmes by model-turned-nutritionist Rose Ferguson and skincare products and treatments by New York’s favourite plastic surgeon, Lara Devgan. Trainers at the futuristic gym can go deep on biohacking or the function of the vagus nerve. In marbled treatment rooms that come with gorgeous floral Alice Temperley robes, guests can have four-hands massages with adaptogenic chakra oils and transcendent sounds. I had a sublime Ashiatsu massage, for which ex-Berkeley therapist Violetta Choma walked across my back, using gymnastic-like grips in the ceiling to change pressure, while using both feet and hands to ease out tensions.
The neighborhood
This is prime London hotel territory, with Knightsbridge just a quick hop to the west and Buckingham Palace a stroll east. But the real star of The Emory is Hyde Park, which is visible from so many spots and surprisingly big from higher vantage points.
The service
Staff are slick for such a new place, but then so many come from Maybourne—starting with smooth guest services manager Josh Shearman, formerly of The Berkeley. So too at ABC kitchens, where the staff are excellent, led by Mike Walker, formerly of The Ivy, 5 Hertford Street, and Rules. At this rarefied level, the London staffing crisis is less of an issue. And every guest has their own Emory Assistant, on call at all times, so service doesn’t get more all-encompassing.
Who comes here?
More than The Claridge’s and The Connaught, this one feels aimed at a global elite: the sort that really do wear Loro Piana caps.
For families
Each floor can be made interconnecting, allowing families up to eight interlinking suites; and the Emory Assistant can organize almost whatever kids want.
Eco effort
Maybourne has just entered into an agreement by which all its London hotels will be powered by 100% renewable electricity—wind power until October, and then by a newly built solar farm in Essex. The group is working towards a recycling rate of 90% across the hotels, and has rigorous policies around suppliers, right down to the shoes worn by Surrenne staff—Hylo trainers, made from recycled broken bottles.
Accessibility
The Emory is fully compliant with the UK's Disability Discrimination Act (DDA). There are three adaptable suites, a wheelchair-friendly elevator, and a pool elevator.
Anything else to mention?
Architect Ivan Harbour, an associate of Richard Rogers, likened the building to “watchmaking on a grand scale, [reflecting a] meticulous attention to detail, where the beauty of movement is apparent, and nothing is hidden.” Hence, the journalist Nicholas Foulkes has curated a library of watch books for The Emory and will invite visiting experts to talk about luxury timepieces for the hotel.
Worth it?
People will talk about the stratospheric pricing of London hotels. And there will still be those who would rather have a quintessentially London experience at Claridge’s. But nowhere in the city
feels
like luxury quite the way The Emory does.
