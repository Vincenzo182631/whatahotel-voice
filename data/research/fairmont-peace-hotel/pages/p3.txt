Reviewed by

Why book?
A stay here is steeped in Shanghai (and world) history. Perhaps the city’s most famous hotel is also a tourist attraction with crowds gathering outside to snap photos or clamoring for a reservation to enjoy afternoon tea. Cocktails at the jazz bar require plenty of advance notice to score a table, but guests always take priority (yet another reason to check in here). But the main reason to stay: a front-row seat to old-world hospitality and glamor.
Set the scene
Tuxedoed gentlemen stand at the entrances to escort guests to the reception or the restaurant. Gilded ceilings, stained glass, and a hushed tone fill the massive lobby and public corridors, all lined with historic art, bas relief panels showing historic scenes of the Bund and oodles of fresh flower bouquets. You almost feel like you’re stepping back in time to an era where formality and dignity were the norm. This is the kind of place where you dress a step above the norm and feel proud to do so.
The guest list here, like at many historic Fairmont hotels around the world, skews heavily toward leisure guests who want to stay where the world’s top diplomats and celebrities have. Thanks to in-the-know door staff, the hotel remains delightfully free of camera-toting tourists.
The backstory
Almost a century old and once known as The Cathay Hotel, the property has been at the forefront of Shanghai history. Its position along the Bund, referred to in the 1930s as the “Wall Street of the East,” has made it a landmark in the city.
Designed by Sir Victor Sassoon, it has stood the test of time, welcoming diplomats, artists, and celebrities, decade after decade. When it opened, it had air conditioning, real silver bathroom faucets, the first functioning hotel toilets in town, Art Deco friezes, Lalique glass fixtures, and a live jazz band that became a hallmark of Shanghai entertainment. When the first of the two buildings opened, it had two electric elevators, another first for Shanghai, and each building became the tallest on the Bund at its inauguration.
The glass dove sculpture beneath the stained glass dome that dominates the main hall was a gift to the hotel and represents peace. That’s a theme that runs throughout the hotel ever since the Chinese government changed the name to the Peace Hotel after the International Symposium on World Peace, a portion of which took place here in 1956.
Aside from several Hollywood films being set onsite, other important events have taken place at the hotel, like the surrender of the Japanese to the Chinese during World War II at the hotel’s Peace Ballroom on the eighth floor, and the engagement festivities for Chiang Kai-shek and Soong Mei-ling. The property has also welcomed a long roster of international figures like past Presidents Bill Clinton, Barack Obama, and Richard Nixon; former US Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice; former Prime Minister of Canada Justin Trudeau; playwright George Bernard Shaw; and actor Charlie Chaplin, among many others.
To celebrate its centenary, the hotel will close for a complete interior makeover in 2026 (although its historic elements will remain), elevating its standards even further to push it one rung higher within the Accor portfolio, moving from Fairmont to the posh Raffles brand.
The rooms
As with any historic hotel, the rooms were not designed specifically for epic city views or to compete with what would become more modern properties around it. Standard rooms, divided between a pair of buildings, face the interior and can seem dim at times. Larger Deluxe rooms look out towards the street. Any of the prized rooms at the end of the building facing the Bund command a steep increase in price, but are worth every penny (RNB).
The old-world architectural charm does not come at the expense of modern perks. While the building has aged well, the interiors are no antique. Fairmont has decked them out in bright colors with floral carpeting, pastel wall coverings and artwork, two-poster beds with every conceivable power outlet within reach, and mahogany cabinetry. Everything oozes with sophistication as you step out of the elevators and pass by historic artifacts like a grand piano or clothes worn by past employees (each with signs indicating the role it has played in the hotel’s past). Famous guests who have slept inside are often listed on plaques outside the door.
Bathrooms vary based on the size of the room, but have excellent water pressure with separate glass showers and soaking tubs, plus Maison Caulieres beauty products in eco-friendly pump bottles. In the suite category, there are several one-bedroom options that close off the sitting area for extra privacy. Big-wig travelers and those on an expense account splurge for one of the Nine Nations Suites, on floors seven to nine, each themed around a particular country. In its early days, these were the original hotel’s guest-room floors, with lower levels dedicated to retail and office space. These suites enjoy the most prestigious, front-facing position with Bund views.
My Indian Suite looked like a royal palace with a pointed Moghul arch, ornate ceilings, a dining table with Indian antiques, separate sitting and sleeping rooms with traditional furnishings, and a massive marble bathroom. The shower, like all of the other Nine Nations Suites, featured windows that looked out towards the Oriental Pearl Tower and all of Pudong, plus the constant traffic along the Huangpu River. This was truly a unique way to start the day.
Fairmont Gold guests enjoy elevated ensuite amenities plus access to a ninth-floor lounge for private check-in, complimentary breakfast and all-day refreshments, plus an evening happy hour with an open bar and a hot buffet. While the lounge has no view, it is a quiet place to catch up on work at any time of day.
With the arrival of Raffles in late 2027, the overall room count will shrink to make way for more suites. All accommodations will see a total overhaul with new fabrics, furnishings, and signature Raffles amenities.
Food and drink
Once Sassoon’s private banquet hall, the Cathay Room on the ninth floor is a decadent dining room serving elevated French fare. Its 270-degree sky garden and Bund-facing balcony are popular photo spots from morning to night. This is where afternoon tea is also served, making it one of the most panoramic in the city.
One floor below is Dragon Phoenix, the Cantonese restaurant at both lunch and dinner, and most popular with locals. On the lobby level, Sunday jazz afternoon tea with dancing takes place at Jasmine Lounge, which also serves the impressive breakfast spread. Numerous live cooking stations and a dim sum spread worthy of royalty await guests each morning. Pan-Asian food is served in Victor’s Café facing Nanjing Road, which is the only food and beverage outlet that welcomes non-guests without a reservation.
And then there’s the jazz band, world-famous for its music and its musicians. It holds a Guinness World Record for having the world’s oldest musicians working together in a band (their average age is in the mid-80s, yet they perform as if they were three decades younger). A singer joins them throughout their evening concert as tipplers enjoy cocktails from the bar; reservations are a must if you want to get a table.
The spa
An indoor pool is on level one, part of the Willow Stream Spa on the same floor. It is home to a wide range of massage and beauty treatments (the Chinese bamboo roll massage comes highly recommended). The fitness center has plentiful free weights and cardio machines. But who could miss jogging along the Bund just outside the front door? That’s certainly a better way to boost endorphins while taking in the scenery.
The neighborhood/area
The front door of the hotel opens to Nanjing Street, home to popular shopping and dining. It buzzes from morning to night, but the rooms are well-soundproofed to protect against any disturbance. And the Huangpu River and the city’s famous Bund promenade are right outside the property.
The service
Male staff don tuxedos; the women wear elegant Chinese outfits. The service here is second to none in the city. Even the cleaning staff stops to say hello when anyone passes. Training here clearly goes above and beyond to match the lofty standards of this famous address, and English is a strong suit.
For families
Children will enjoy the pool and endless distraction along Nanjing Road. The option to connect one-bedroom suites with a neighboring room is popular with families. But in general, this is not a family-focused hotel, and the hushed interiors of this hotel and its restaurants are not ideal for frolicking kiddos.
Anything left to mention?
The hotel museum is worth perusing and is free for guests. Guided tours are available free for guests by reservation, but outsiders must pay for the experience.
