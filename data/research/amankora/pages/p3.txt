Why book?
A stay at Amankora is a slow pilgrimage circuit through Bhutan, broken across five lodges among the Himalayas. You don't have to do all five to get the idea—I stayed at the two most popular, Thimphu and Paro. The journey moves through rice paddies, pine forests, and sacred highlands, with every lodge receiving you the same way: "welcome home."
Set the scene
The kingdom of Bhutan—a small, landlocked Buddhist nation set between India and China—opened to international tourism only a half-century ago. Today, foreign visitors pay a $100 daily fee as part of a “high value, low impact” model to preserve the country's distinctive culture and fragile natural environment. Rhododendrons and pine groves carpet the valleys. Hilltop dzongs (fortresses that double as administrative and spiritual centers) are Bhutan's skyscrapers. Happiness is measured here like GDP is measured elsewhere.
Each of the five Amankora lodges, with 8 to 24 suites, is shaped by the region it’s in. There is urban-adjacent seclusion in the capital, Thimphu; a taste of riverside farmhouse life in the rice paddies of Punakha; crane-watching and yak-spotting in high-altitude Gangtey; temple-hopping through the spiritual heartland of Bumthang; and forest bathing beneath a 17th-century fortress ruin in Paro.
Every lodge runs signature activities like prayer-flag making and archery (which is the national sport), drawing a mix of well-heeled couples on honeymoon and multigenerational families vacationing with their adult children. There are “Amanjunkies” working their way through the portfolio mingling with casually dressed first-timers. The common thread: a tolerance for long transfers to get here.
The backstory
Modern travel in Bhutan and the Aman brand have a shared origin story. It starts in 1989. The first-ever commercial flight from Thailand landed in Paro, which dramatically expanded the kingdom’s reach. That same year, Aman launched its debut hotel, Amanpuri, also in Thailand. The property’s name combined two Sanskrit words,
aman
for peace and
puri
for place. The formula stuck, and every Aman since has followed it.
In Bhutan, the second word is
kora
, meaning "circular pilgrimage" in Dzongkha, the Bhutanese language. Amankora, as it became known, arrived in 2004 as the first international hospitality brand ever permitted to operate in the country. The late Kerry Hill was brought in to design the lodges in the government-mandated style of whitewashed rammed-earth walls and wooden-framed windows. Hill, the longtime Aman architect also behind Amanpuri and Aman Tokyo, created a pared-back version of the typical ornately decorated buildings. The Paro lodge opened first, with the full five-property circuit completed three years later.
While the lodges have all received significant upgrades in the years since, the Paro and Punakha locations will both be closed through September 2026 for extensive renovations. Once reopened, Punakha will debut an expanded spa, a new yoga pavilion in the rice fields, and a reimagined 300-year-old farmhouse. Paro will add a hammam spa house and a refreshed main restaurant, complementing its recently opened Iglu Sauna—a riverside treatment space inspired by Eastern European banya tradition.
The rooms
Almost all 76 suites across the five lodges share a single template. Each accommodation is an open-plan bedroom and living area of 560 square feet with Aman's signature deep-cushioned king-size bed; a banquette window seat for watching mist move in; a traditional bukhari wood-burning stove; and a terrazzo-clad bathroom which features a freestanding tub and walk-in rain shower. The one exception is at Punakha, which has two Mo Chhu Suites, which can be combined to form a two-bedroom configuration, making this setup the best pick for families traveling together or couples craving more room to spread out.
There are no televisions in any room, nor a mini-bar. It’s a reminder that you're here for what's outside the window, not the usual trappings. The bathroom layout is distinctly for solo travelers or couples. Double vanities are a nice touch, but the exposed tub and an open shower make the setup less comfortable for friends or a family sharing the room. On the plus side, the heated vanity with adjustable temperature settings is heavenly on chilly mornings.
Food and drink
Eating in Bhutan means eating a lot of chili, cheese, and rice—often all in the same bowl. The lodges remain rooted in that tradition but also serve Western classics and fusion dishes, along with a roster of signature off-lodge dining experiences. There's an alfresco dinner in Thimphu, where the meal is prepared over an open flame beside a forest stream, or a log cabin lunch at the base of Tiger's Nest, reserved for guests returning from the hike to Bhutan's holiest monastery.
Over four nights across Paro and Thimphu, my standout dining experience was lunch at that log cabin. The two-room cottage offers sweeping views of the monastery, which clings to a cliff high above the valley floor. After a nearly 2,000-foot ascent and descent, I was met with a coconut welcome drink, followed by a decadent Bhutanese spread of momos, ema datshi (chili and cheese), red rice, pork with chilies, and seasonal vegetables. When I sat down, the staff swapped my trail-dusted sneakers for slippers. Then, after the meal, my shoes came back—sparkling clean.
At each of the lodge restaurants, the menu structure is identical: an à la carte of Western options and fusion offerings, plus a rotating set menu that alternates between Bhutanese and another Asian cuisine (Indian at Paro, Thai at Thimphu). Most ingredients come from local farmers and the lodges' own kitchen gardens, with specialty items like Bumthang honey sourced directly. Breads and dips rotate at every meal, too—ezay, a ubiquitous Bhutanese chili condiment made with tangy local tree tomatoes, was especially memorable.
Food and beverages are all-inclusive, with an extensive drink list that covers local Bhutanese beers and whisky (only top-shelf spirits cost extra). There's plenty for nondrinkers, too. I enjoyed a spicy, house-made kombucha with chili and ginger. Don’t miss suja, or black tea churned with butter and salt, which shows up everywhere from afternoon monastery visits to morning breakfast spreads. It's savory and nourishing. Dessert isn't a traditional part of Bhutanese cuisine, so most local meals end with fresh fruit (or more tea). Amankora's rotating dessert list includes homemade ice cream, fruit-forward sweets, and the occasional Bhutanese riff.
The spa
Every Amankora lodge has a spa which draws on traditional Bhutanese healing practices. The signature treatment is the dotsho, or hot stone bath, a ritual locals have practiced for generations to ease joint pain, arthritis, and the toll of long winters spent farming. River stones are warmed in an open fire, then lowered into a separate chamber of a wooden tub; heat transfers through a slatted divider, and minerals seep into the water alongside fresh khempa, a local artemisia herb.
After the Tiger's Nest hike, I headed straight to Paro's spa for my 60-minute dotsho. Each of Paro's five treatment rooms opens onto its own private outdoor stone bath, lit by candles in the evening. I jumped in and waited for the stones to crackle loudly as they hit the water. After four hours up and down a mountain, it was deeply restorative.
For travelers on the lodge circuit, stays of seven nights or more include one 60-minute treatment per guest. Complimentary morning yoga and sound baths are also on the schedule, and optional monk-led meditation sessions can be arranged for guests seeking a more immersive experience.
The neighborhood/area
Amankora's lodges sit across five of Bhutan's most iconic valleys, carved into the eastern Himalayas and separated by high mountain passes. A single, winding east-west road connects them; traveling in Bhutan requires some tolerance for motion sickness. The drive from Paro to Thimphu takes about an hour, then another three hours to Punakha. Any trip to the kingdom requires a licensed guide and driver, which Amankora can arrange, but isn't required to book through. Mine were Changa and Eugyen, and they were sharp on local history and extremely skilled on the mountain roads.
A handful of newer properties now offer their own takes on the circuit model, but Amankora pioneered it in 2004. Each lodge sits near the doorstep of whatever its region is best known for. In Paro, Tiger's Nest (the country's signature hike) is a quick drive away, as is the National Museum of Bhutan, housed inside a 17th-century watchtower. Watching landings at the airport is also an attraction in and of itself, with one of the most dramatic approaches in the world. A final descent is between 18,000-foot peaks. Meanwhile, in Thimphu, Bhutan's biggest city and the world's only capital without traffic lights, the lodge sits in a vast forest just above town, a short drive from the towering Buddha Dordenma statue that keeps watch over the valley.
The service
After more than two decades in Bhutan, Amankora's operation runs seamlessly. Staff in traditional Bhutanese dress—gho for men, kira for women—move through the property with choreographed precision, though interactions can sometimes verge on feeling scripted.
In typical Aman fashion, service is incredibly anticipatory and proactive. Even when I left my room for 15 minutes for an afternoon stroll, I'd return to find my bed re-made and fresh water on the nightstand. Coordination carries across the circuit, too, with preferences noted at one lodge following you to the next.
In Paro, I was dropped off at what appeared to be the side of the road. None of the lodges have a conventional lobby or check-in desk, nor any signage. A path with pine needles underfoot led me deeper into the forest, and a lime-washed stone pavilion emerged. I was greeted the same way guests are each time they set foot onto Amankora, as if I were coming home.
Eco effort
Sustainability is a lower bar in Bhutan than in most places. The country is already carbon-negative, over 70 percent forested (the national constitution requires at least 60 percent to be preserved), and powered almost entirely by hydroelectricity. Guided experiences route guests directly to local artisans and monasteries, keeping tourism dollars close to the communities that host them. At Amankora, lodges were built with rammed-earth walls, local timber, and, in part, yak hair. Kitchen gardens at every lodge now supply a significant share of the produce.
Anything left to mention?
Springtime and autumn months are the best windows to visit. Summer monsoons can wash out hikes, while winter brings too much cold for the trails. Daily laundry is included in the nightly rate, which means you shouldn't overpack. If you're short on time or can’t handle winding mountain drives, Amankora can arrange helicopter transfers between lodges (for an added cost) or a 35-minute domestic flight from Paro to Bumthang.
