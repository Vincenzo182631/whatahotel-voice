Alila Shanghai: First In
A stay in the heart of Shanghai that brilliantly combines the city's old-world atmosphere with its cosmopolitan edge

Reviewed by

Set the scene
Hidden down an unassuming driveway off central
Shanghai
's Weihai Road, Alila Shanghai's dimly lit foyer marks the transition between the city's high-octane streets to a hushed hideaway of earthy tones, natural materials, and lots of greenery. Take the elevator up to the fourth-floor lobby, and you arrive in a light-flooded courtyard in the sky, a mesh of swirling pathways, mirror-flat waterworks, and manicured gardens surrounding a 17-foot tree made from ceramic and stainless steel by local artist Oscar Wang. The artwork's title,
Gathering,
is apt: from the cozy tea lounge to the glass-walled restaurants and private dining rooms circling the courtyard, the hotel is as much a place to sleep as it is a downtown rendezvous for well-heeled Shanghainese on date nights, business lunches and family gatherings.
The backstory
If you've visited
Shanghai
before, the hotel's shell might look familiar: before Alila took over this 37-floor tower in the Jing’an neighborhood, the building flew the Four Seasons flag. A five-year, top-to-bottom refurbishment spearheaded by
Beijing
-based interior designer Ju Bin saw the amount of rooms reduced from 439 to 186 (part of the building is now a shopping mall), while the interiors shed their somewhat dated designs for a fresh look that riffs on traditional Chinese courtyard homes and the East-meets-West style of old Shanghai's shikumen houses. The result is fresh and breezy, with a strong sense of place that doesn't lean on any tropes. As Alila's first urban outpost in China, it perfectly captures the resort-style hotel concept the brand is known for.
The rooms
Earth-toned, straight-lined and blissfully spacious: the hotel's 186 guest rooms (of which 94 are suites) offer a welcome retreat from the cacophonous streets below. Like the communal areas, their design draws on old-timey Shanghai, with blonde wood marquetry, rattan wall panels, and ceilings bedecked in earthy stucco. In the open-plan bathrooms, geometric floor tiles—often found in the city's classic mansions—add a playful pop of retro, while contemporary paper lanterns and panels of vintage-style textured glass deliver a similarly nostalgic hit. Most rooms have a deep-set sofa lining the full length of the window, but views vary depending on the floor and rooms' direction—aim for an East-facing room on a high floor if you want a glimpse of The Bund's
Blade Runner
skyline on the horizon.
Food and drink
One of Asia's
greatest food cities
unfurls on your doorstep, so you'll have no problem eating well around the city. But with four bars and restaurants, Alila offers plenty of reasons to stay in. Mornings start at Garden Pavilion, an all-day diner surrounding a glass-walled courtyard where a single Japanese maple tree takes center stage. A buffet of fresh fruits and creative pastries (such as chocolate-banana croissants) complement a range of classic a la carte options and Japanese, European, and Chinese set menus. Do try the Shanghainese set with youtiao dough crullers, warm soy milk, and sesame peanut butter noodles at least once during your stay, and keep room for some shumai and xiao long bao dumplings from the bamboo steamer-cart rolled around the room from time to time.
For lunch and dinner, the Garden Pavilion turns into a Japanese robatayaki grill, but for something a bit more rooted in place, you'll want to seek out 500 Weihai Road, the hotel's signature restaurant. Here, chef Scott Bao (who formerly cooked at Huangzhou's 28 Hubin Road), serves up contemporary and seafood-heavy riffs on classic Shanghainese dishes, with highlights including the sea cucumber marinated in Shaoxing rice wine; the steamed mud crab, and sweet, melt-in-the-mouth beef ribs.
The library-like Chayan@5th tea parlor deals in more than a dozen types of organic Chinese teas, from Dongding Oolong to raw Pu'Er, which it serves alongside a small menu of cakes and pastries. More fun, though, are the tea-infused cocktails—my silky smooth Earl Grey Martini had me ordering a second round. The Secret Roof hotel bar, with a terrazzo-clad rooftop terrace, overlooks the terracotta-roofed shikumen neighborhood just across the street.
The spa
The Alila brand is big on wellness, and this Shanghai outpost is no exception. A wellness center, Spa Alila, takes over almost the entire fifth floor, and includes a sprawling gym (accessible around the clock), a yoga room, and a hydrotherapy area with sauna and Turkish bath. In the spa's timber-clad treatment rooms, sessions fuse muscle-melting massages with ancient Asian therapies such as sound healing with Tibetan singing bowls and foot baths with herbs picked from the organic garden on the roof terrace. The heated pool, with two sides of floor-to-ceiling windows framing Shanghai's glittering cityscape, is another highlight, but do note that you'll need to wear a swimming cap before jumping in (which is customary in much of China).
The neighborhood/area
The hotel's downtown perch puts much of the city within easy reach: the sprawling Taikoo Hui mall sits just across the street, while Zhangyuan, a commercial complex of shikumen homes that have been revamped as restaurants, cafes, and high-end fashion boutiques is around the corner. The Bund, Shanghai's skyscraper-bristled postcard sight, is a short taxi-hop (or easy, 30-minute stroll) away, and transfer from Shanghai's Pudong Airport takes about an hour.
The service
The young team, mostly local with a few foreigners mixed in, was unfailingly friendly and eager to please, though the service in the restaurants wasn't always as polished as at some of Alila's more classic five-star peers. It could've been a little more assertive (empty plates lingered on tables for a while; we constantly had to ask for menus, water, and cutlery), but I'm sure that with a few more months on the job, that'll be smoothed out.
For families
Even the entry-level rooms have plenty of space for an extra bed (or a couch to sleep on), while some rooms can be combined into a two-bedroom family suite. There are no kids menus in the restaurants and there's no kids club, but my six-year-old still felt right at home.
Eco effort
The hotel's refurbishment followed the EarthCheck Design standard, which means that, where possible, the architects and designers used sustainable building practices. Rainwater is collected and repurposed to irrigate gardens and clean outside spaces, while strategic landscaping and the use of materials with a High Solar Reflectance Index (SRI) reduce the urban heat island effect. Food scraps and coffee from the kitchen turn into fertilizer for the gardens.
Accessibility
All restaurants and communal areas have ramps and elevator access for guests with mobility issues, and there's one room with increased accessibility features, such as widened pathways for wheelchair users.
Anything left to mention?
Alila's cultural programming is another highlight that's soon to roll out more extensively. Exclusively available for in-house guests, its rooted-in-Shanghai activities (some free, others at a charge) include early-morning gong sound therapy sessions in the spa, therapeutic incense-making workshops at a traditional local incense atelier, and a Chinese Medicine master class in partnership with a clinic that dates back to 1734.
