Amanzoe
Marble-clad villas and nods to the Acropolis define this hotel, overlooking the glitz of Porto Heli Bay.

Reviewed by
Rachel Howard
Why book?
If you’re feeling run-down, stressed out, or overwhelmed, this place is a natural healer. The zen vibe is a little too staged to be sexy; but if you want to give yourself space to think, rest, or totally decompress, you’ve hit the spot.
Set the scene
Deliberately designed like a modern-day Acropolis, Amanzoe is poised on a hilltop with wide-screen views of the rolling valleys and smooth seas of the Peloponnese. A sense of exclusivity is built into the walled grounds, where the stillness is so profound that you often feel as though you have the entire place to yourself. The only sounds in your private pavilion are birdsong and a symphony of critters as nature rubs up against the swaths of pale stone and marble.
Although this is billed as a perfect place to shy away from paparazzi, some guests can’t resist flaunting their lifestyles (and abs) on Instagram. Sunset selfies in thigh-high taffeta and heels and a parade of designer swimwear by the pool are perfectly acceptable. But you could just as comfortably wander around the rosemary-scented grounds in the flip-flops and kitten-soft bathrobes provided without causing a stir. Sure, there are probably supermodels, sports stars, and famous actors in your midst (don’t stare). But mostly it’s a crowd of good-looking couples radiating a post-coital, pre-baby glow; paunchy CEOs and their toned wives; and improbably fit 50-somethings saluting the sun in the yoga pavilion.
The backstory
This is Aman, so it’s all resolutely anti-bling and anti-clutter—with minimalist design that merges with its surroundings and four staffers to every guest. The staff are prohibited from name-dropping famous guests, but social media has it that Rita Ora, Lily Aldridge, and Cristiano Ronaldo have all checked into Amanzoe.
Rooms
Apart from the views and the size of your private pools, all 38 pavilions (nothing as basic as rooms here) are very similar: pale and interesting, decked in enough gray and white marble to empty a quarry and much bigger than the average Parisian apartment. Double-height ceilings and sliding wooden doors that open onto a walled, shaded terrace and plunge pool add to the extravagant sense of space. Greek touches abound—marble busts, dried figs, and pomegranate soda in the complimentary minibar, a copy of Euripides’ Bacchae on the coffee table (a nod to the ancient theater of Epidaurus nearby). Huge ‘his and hers’ dressing rooms and separate bathrooms (with those terrifying Japanese loos) flank a sunken marble bathtub, where you can lie back by candlelight and gaze outdoors at the subtly lit shrubs and olive trees. You see nothing but nature from the huge bed, which is simply dressed in softest white, with pillows that yield to the touch and lighting that segues from dim to ambient at the touch of a button. Deluxe pavilions come with 12-meter pools and the best views over the olive groves to the sea. If you prefer to wake up to the sound of waves, book one of the four cabanas by the beach club (five miles from the resort).
Food and drink
Given that there are no restaurants in the vicinity, you’ll end up eating most of your meals in one of the three restaurants. Breakfast is à la carte, with page after page of options, from classic Greek yogurt with honeycomb and walnuts to Cretan-style eggs Benedict with wild greens, apaki (smoked pork), and hollandaise, all sorts of juices and smoothies, and bottomless pots of matcha or Greek mountain tea. There’s less of a local or seasonal touch in the main Mediterranean restaurant: Apart from a few token Greek dishes (stuffed cabbage leaves, lamb chops), the menu is more about premium ingredients that will please anyone with expensive tastes: a gooey blob of burrata with tart green tomatoes and basil oil; a zingy tuna and avocado tartare; premium cuts of Australian, Uruguayan, and American steak, and a pricey wine list (try one of the Nemea reds, produced nearby).
At Nama, the smaller, poolside Japanese restaurant, every dish is prettily plated but a little short on authenticity. The easygoing snack menu at the Beach Club tastes more real: an artful pile of Greek salad topped with pickled samphire, served with focaccia buns for scooping up the feta, or filleted sardines with foraged sea greens. It’s all perfectly executed—just don’t expect taverna prices. At least you can raid the cereal bars, pink grapefruit soda, and beers in the minibar without worrying about the consequences: it’s replenished daily and free of charge.
The service
There’s nothing as crass as a check-in desk here. A welcoming committee in beige and white stands to attention on the marble staircase, framed by shiny black amphorae and column after column receding into the horizon. An elixir of citrus-and-honey and a cool towel are pressed into your hands. After a quick tour of the property and your pavilion, the young, slightly self-conscious staff leave you in blissful peace.
The service ethos is more discreet deference than chatty confidence. For the most part, the staff is almost invisible (irksomely so at the Beach Club, where a bottle of iced water would be welcome on arrival). Yet leave your room for a few moments and it will miraculously have been spruced up on your return, your linen slippers realigned, and an evil eye charm placed upon your pillow. Ask for something—anything—and it will be taken care of. But if you prefer to keep a low profile, you won’t be disturbed.
The neighborhood/area
Don’t come here looking for a party. There’s no nightlife, hip dining scene, or cool shopping (the on-site boutique is airport lounge chic). The beauty of Amanzoe is its isolation. The quietness of the landscape and the absence of distractions or irritations is an instant balm. If you want some local color, it’s a 15-minute drive to Kilada, a cute fishing port with some fine tavernas, and a thrilling speedboat (or less thrilling hydrofoil) ride to the glamorous islands of Hydra and Spetses, where moneyed Athenians have weekend homes. Into ancient history? The fourth century BC sanctuary of Asclepius and amphitheater of Epidaurus (where outdoor performances take place in summer) is less than an hour’s drive; glorious Mycenae is 90 minutes by car.
Anything you’d change?
The calm atmosphere occasionally borders on soporific. The entertainment could be a little racier than being serenaded by a trio singing Greek power ballads at dinnertime. A little more seasonal variety on the menu wouldn’t go amiss.
Anything left to mention?
One visit to the spa is never enough. Therapists glide through plant-filled colonnades to vast, sky-lit treatment rooms scented with orange blossom oil, where they’ll rub, knead, and polish you anew. Grounding, purifying, and nourishing treatments go way beyond skin-deep and really leave you feeling different. Even if you’re the kind of person (like me) who hates gyms, exercise feels like a pleasure in the glass-walled Pilates studio and yoga pavilion, overlooking an expanse of lurid green valleys and dusky hills fading into the horizon. (Take advantage of the free exercise classes a few times a week).
Shiny black cars and mountain bikes are on standby to purr you down a three-mile country road to the Beach Club, where the mood is even more drowsy and low-key (though kids and alpha males will enjoy messing about with the paddleboards, windsurfers, and other non-motorised water-sports). Striped loungers are lined up by two shimmering green marble pools, or you can jump off a wooden jetty into the sea or a waiting powerboat. There are stunning coves in abundance on this lush stretch of Peloponnesian coastline.
