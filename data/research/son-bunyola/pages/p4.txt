Son Bunyola
Richard Branson returns to the Balearic island with a sustainably minded finca offering unbeatable coast views and an intent to revive the area’s ecosystem.

Reviewed by

Why book?
This has been one of Richard Branson’s passion projects for decades, and it’s finally been completed: a lovingly reimagined 16th century finca set amid 1,300 acres of olive groves and vineyards with teetering views down to the sea and the Tramuntana mountains rising behind. This is Mallorca at its wildest and most original—a
UNESCO World Heritage Site
no less—and Son Bunyola places you right smack bang in the heart of the landscape. Open in June 2023, it’s hit the ground running, creating—like all the best hotels—a believable world of its own.
Set the scene
Up in the Tramuntana mountains, the scent of rosemary in the air, goats jingle-jangling their bells and a solitary eagle riding the thermals, it’s easy to forget about the millions of visitors who descend on Mallorca every summer. This is the island’s rugged north-west coast and its history is etched on the landscape, from neolithic remains to the dry-stone walls and terraces built by the Moors to grow almonds and olives, and medieval watchtowers looking out to sea—the building boom of the 1960s, thankfully, never made inroads here. Son Bunyola occupies a natural amphitheater, with an extensive terrace looking down over a small village to the bay below and the striking Sa Foradada rock formation: to reach this view try walking in a straight line through the hotel’s entrance, courtyard, and bar and then out to a circular fountain—it’s a single-shot cinematic moment of pure hotel drama.
Inside the finca is the original olive press, the chapel’s altarpiece takes pride of place in the main restaurant, and the 13th-century defensive tower, rising above the tiled courtyard, now protects two suites. The
swimming pool
, lined with turquoise tiles and shimmering in the sunlight, is deserving of a Slim Aarons photograph or two. During my sneak peek, Branson is omnipresent, ploughing lengths in the pool, cracking top spins over the net during a mini championship with two tennis pros, and leading a cycling peloton up hill and down valley—him on a road bike, everyone else on e-bikes.
The backstory
Back in 1987, the same year he completed the first crossing of the Atlantic in a hot-air balloon, Branson bought La Residencia after a former girlfriend tipped him off about a neglected but beautiful house near Deià on Mallorca’s northern coast. It became one of the boutique hotels of the era, tapping into the region’s bohemian reputation and drawing guests such as Princess Diana and Andrew Lloyd Webber. But Branson also had designs on another estate nearby, Son Bunyola, which he bought in 1994, creating three standalone villas on the land before selling it eight years later after failing to get a license to develop the original finca. Fast forward to 2015 and the entrepreneur was offered the land back—and this time he was given the green light to proceed with his plans. The estate itself dates back to the 13th century, when the original tower was built and the vineyards, almond orchards and olive groves planted, although the finca itself is 16th century and the round tower built in the 1930s. By the 1970s it had gone to seed, though some of the hotel staff lived here as children then, helping farm alfalfa, almonds and vegetables. It’s taken two years of work by Spanish-based Gras Architects (which also worked on the new Kimpton Mallorca) to bring the finca back to life, restoring lime paint, stone arches and coffered ceilings, while Swedish-Mallorcan design studio Rialto Living focused on the interiors, adding Riviera stripes, lobster pinks, and Mallorcan artworks alongside existing antiques, with Eva Branson’s Morocco foundation weaving many of the textiles.
The rooms
Just 27 in all, little cool-air enclaves stirred by ceiling fans and with the feeling of being deliciously tucked away while the house party below continues into the small hours. You wake up and flick aside the white cotton drapes to let the sunshine stream in. Many have original wooden rafters, and are mostly set around the courtyard with views over the pool or mountains; my room, 28, had its own sitting room with brass pendants and sofas. Others have their own terrace; there are two such suites alongside the olive press, with steps up to the rooftop terrace, and two more in the 13th-century tower. The design is restful and in keeping with the island aesthetic—plenty of taupe, blues and greens—though doesn’t surprise in any way. Rooms come with yoga mats.
Food and drink
Extremadura-born chef Samuel Galdón worked at the original La Residencia and at his own Michelin-starred restaurant in Deia, so knows Mallorca’s terroir well. He says his menus will be vegetable-forward, shaking the trees and bushes for ingredients, most of which are grown on the estate or the island—for lunch, under canvas awnings on the terrace, we scooped up sharing plates of hummus and coca bread; tomatoes, figs, and burrata; for dinner a delicate bowl of mussels and samphire, and thinly sliced cucumber and scallops, pretty as an O’Keefe picture, grilled leeks and hazelnuts, and Iberican pork and lobster from the charcoal grill. There are plans to pickle and ferment, to prevent food waste. A second restaurant opens in July in the original olive press, with Mallorcan spins on tapas on the menu. Meanwhile, behind the zinc counter of the bar, Jason shakes island mojitos using Mallorcan rum.
The neighborhood
Around 40 minutes north of Palma, the Tramuntana mountains are Mallorca’s go-slow region of thick forests and high peaks, the winding roads illuminated by the occasional flash of lycra as a cyclist whizzes past (Mallorca’s long been on the map for serious riders, thanks to slow-burn climbs such as Col Soller and the sheer safety of the roads). This is also the Mallorca of bohemians and dreamers, where Chopin and George Sand argued and poet Robert Graves carved out his Deià enclave in the 1930s (more prosaically, Michael Douglas and Catherine Zeta-Jones have their holiday home here). Nearby are quiet villages such as Esporles and Banyalbufar, while Valldemossa and Soller are a little further afield—a nice day out on one of the hotel’s e-bikes. From the empty pebble beach below the hotel (watch out for nudists) you can swim, kayak, and picnic, while a painting or photography masterclass will connect you to the landscape.
The service
Mostly Spanish, with many from Mallorca, the team are likable and down to earth, working under the watchful eye of general manager Vincent Padioleau, who worked at La Residencia before Branson sold it to Belmond. One of the housekeeping staff is an accomplished flamenco dancer —he might be persuaded to show you a move or two.
Eco effort
There are several easy-tick initiatives here, from filtered water in the rooms and using all waste water to irrigate the gardens, along with a biomass boiler and surplus energy from the cooling system used to heat water. But as well as restoring the finca, Son Bunyola has an ongoing mission to revive the landscape around it, planting trees to replace those lost in a recent storm—you’ll notice the slightly barren hillsides and broken trunks—and vineyards that will produce wine from 2026. Around 75 percent of ingredients on the menu come from the estate’s gardens, orchards, and olive groves; along with beehives and a growing clutch of chickens. A fleet of donkeys help keep the estate trimmed.
Accessibility
One of the bedrooms—Room 1—has been designed for wheelchair users; there’s easy access to the restaurant and bar, and to the spa via a lift.
For families
While there aren’t a lot of facilities for children there’s a smaller pool by the main one, and inflatable floats to paddle around on. You can take a golf cart down to the tennis court, and a farm tour to meet the donkeys, peacocks and sheep—though it’s probably better suited for teens who will appreciate the bike trails around the estate and adventure sports in the area.
Anything left to mention?
The original three villas, with private pools, have been updated and are still part of the estate; they can be self-catered although guests can eat in the hotel’s two restaurants. A fourth villa is set to be restored to contain 12 hotel suites, opening in 2025. The hotel spa is small but very accomplished, run by locally based Gaia Natural Products with a short menu of treatments using island oils.
It’s easy to see why Branson’s eye was turned all those years ago and why he persevered with the hotel: the setting and views on this northwest corner of the island are simply astounding (although the grounds and surrounding vineyard will take a while to bed in). Mallorca’s never been short of stylish places to stay, but the north of the island is in focus right now with several new openings; Son Bunyola feels like the most elevated arrival on the island, though—quite literally.
