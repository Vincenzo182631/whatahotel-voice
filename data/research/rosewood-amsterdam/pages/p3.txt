Rosewood Amsterdam
Rosewood's fabled, 10-year-long restoration project in one of Amsterdam's grandest heritage buildings

Reviewed by

Why book?
To finally peek behind the curtain of Rosewood's fabled, 10-year-long restoration project in one of
Amsterdam
's grandest heritage buildings. To enjoy a 1,000-strong collection of art without ever stepping foot in a museum. And to check into a new offering that proves this luxury hotel group still has a few tricks up its sleeve.
Set the scene
From Prinsengracht, one of the four main canals that make up Amsterdam's winding waterway network, Rosewood Amsterdam cuts an imposing figure. The former Palace of Justice is a world away from the majority of the city's top-of-class hotels, many of which have taken over a series of stitched-together Dutch townhouses. Here, a decade-long renovation has restored the property's grand bones, but injected every space (many of which are protected due to the building's historic nature) with modern art, interactive installations and an ever-present sense of fun.
Swept through the doors by dapper door staff, I first notice the gently undulating art at the end of the lobby—a collaboration with Nxt Museum, which will change regularly. Then there's Maarten Baas's
Grandmother Clock
by the check-in area (you can spot another of Baas's gleeful clocks upstairs—another sits in the Rijksmuseum.) A vending machine next to the main staircase is filled with art from visual artist Caspar Braat, who creates smile-inducing Dutch motifs (a cone of chips, a pigeon) from marble. A peek behind a hidden curtain into the gallery—an ever-changing space for pop-ups, exhibitions, and cultural moments—reveals more work from Braat: an immersive garage scene where everything from the broken-down car to the traffic cones is carved from marble.
The backstory
Rosewood Amsterdam has serious history, hence my initial surprise at just how unbuttoned the hotel is. Built in the 17th century as an orphanage, it was renovated to be unrecognizable in the 1820s, after which it became the city's Palace of Justice. For 200 years, it was the site of criminal trials – including that of Cor van Hout, mastermind of the 1980s Freddy Heineken kidnapping – and scandalous protests such as those by Dutch Provo activists, the non-violent forefathers to the later hippie movement (more on them later). In 2013, services were moved, and the building became vacant. Two years later, Rosewood purchased it, with a vision of softening its hallowed halls and extraordinary architecture into something a little
less
severe. The lengthy renovation was partly due to the building's protected status, which required careful restoration and thoughtful modernizing that didn't compromise the original architecture (Rosewood wasn't even allowed to clean the façade). Studio Piet Boon, also behind the interiors at the recently opened Rosewood Resort Miyakojima, was brought in to oversee the designs. The building's history can be felt at every turn, from grand staircases with original tiling to the building's holding cells, which have been turned into an intriguing feature rather than hidden away. There's art in seemingly every nook and cranny, and much of it is immersive, creating a feeling that this is a living, breathing property with secrets to tell.
The rooms
The rooms blend heritage and history with organic textures and natural hues to create spaces that feel soothing rather than formidable. Restrictions on alterations to the moulded ceilings and walls prompted Piet Boon to create boxes within boxes to separate living spaces from bedrooms and bathrooms. Our room had a cosy living area with a plump sofa, desk and coffee-station-slash-mini-bar, which abuts a welcome space to spread out during our stay. Fabric headboards back enormous beds, there's ample closet space, and the enormous bathroom has a free-standing bathtub with Votary products to soothe sore feet after a day pounding the cobbles. Like the rest of the hotel, there's art at every turn. As well as the 134 rooms and suites, there are five ‘houses’, designed to feel like elevated Dutch apartments. These have full kitchens, sweeping lounges and walk-in wardrobes.
Food and drink
I'm not usually a big hotel-restaurant person—dining out is a wonderful way to get under the skin of a city, and I love to try different spots, sometimes squeezing two lunches into one day. But I am converted during lunch at the delightfully feminine Eeuwen, the main restaurant set just off the lobby. We share a flatbread topped with leeks, mushrooms, pickled lemon and straciatella (our server insisted we order this, and he was correct to push), before moving on to a grilled pork chop, served on the bone with just the right amount of rendered fat, cut through with bright wild garlic. That evening, we head for drinks at Advocatuur, the dark and moody hotel bar. This feels more like a neighbourhood hangout than a stiff hotel bar, thanks to its entrance right on the street and the ’90s Europop soundtrack. Staff run us through the Provo-inspired cocktail menu, with suitably proactive names. I try a J'Accuse, basing my choice purely on the name as instructed by the bartender, and am delighted when my favorite type of tipple appears—made with Scotch whisky, barley and chocolate. As we order Indian bites from the bar's tandoor oven, we make our way through a few more of the bar's offerings: the smoky Uprising and the nutty 1829 were personal favourites. As night draws in, we're whisked mysteriously to the cells—not to atone for our bad behaviour, but for a guided tasting of the hotel's own-distilled genever.
The neighbourhood
Set on Prinsengracht in the historic canal district, you can walk pretty much anywhere from Rosewood's front steps—or jump on one of the hotel's bikes. We explored Jordaan, Vondelpark, and De Pijp on foot during our stay.
The service
Rosewood Amsterdam opened ahead of schedule, welcoming guests a full month ahead of its slated opening date. Practically unheard of in the hotel world, but bang on-brand – after all, it’s a long-spun joke that the Dutch are uber-efficient. So it should come as no surprise that every step of our weekend runs on time, from transfers to spa treatments to lunch reservations. But far from simple, ever-prompt robots, doormen are warm, check-in staff compassionate when we arrive in the early hours after a delayed Eurostar service, and waiters in the restaurant know the menu inside-out after just a few days, recommending wine to go with our order without ever being pushy and bringing a second spoon with my partner’s pudding when I insisted I was too full to share. Encouraged by the team to experience Amsterdam outside of the UNESCO-listed center, we hopped aboard a boat with one of Rosewood's dedicated skippers, who whisked us along the waterways to nearby village Durgerdam, plying us with Champagne and alternating between being a well-informed, diligent tour guide and a twinkly-eyed, playful captain.
Wellness & spa
Swaddled below the hotel's sprawling ground floor, Asaya Spa takes its cues from traditional Eastern healing practices and Ayurvedic traditions. Earthy, moody colors cosset wellness-seekers in the treatment rooms, making way for organic cream hues in the diminutive swimming pool. I settled in for a late-afternoon massage, a welcome relief after travel delays and a day of exploring. My therapist seemed to mould my aching limbs like clay, teasing out knots in my back and paying special attention to my extra-tight right shoulder. A few teething issues with sound levels occasionally interrupted my zen state, but I still left feeling delightfully liquid-like. There's also a 24-hour gym, group classes and steam rooms.
Accessibility
The hotel's public areas are all accessible for wheelchair users, including the spa. There are accessible rooms, and staff are trained to advise on specific requests.
Is it worth it?
During our stay, we saw multiple Amsterdam locals poking around, eager to discover what lay behind this façade with almost mythical status. Who can blame them? This is a high-spirited opening for curious neighbors and travelers alike, with a mischievous sense of fun that firmly roots guests in their destination. Do as the Dutch do and join them.
