The St. Regis Bermuda Resort
This is rest and relaxation on repeat: Spend a glorious few days parked on this boomerang of perfect pink sand, where the ocean—and a top-notch cocktail—are never more than a minute away.

Reviewed by
Betsy Blumenthal
Why book?
If you’re aching for an easy but high-caliber dose of down-time—one where you can practically roll out of bed and onto the beach and have a foamy cocktail placed in your hand just a few minutes later—this new St. Regis fits the bill, and then some.
Set the scene:
Away from the buzzy harborfront of Hamilton, the St. Regis’s location—on a quiet beach on St. George’s Island, on the northeast tip of
Bermuda
—feels right on brand for the hotel’s low-key, laidback feel. Far from late-night revelry and shots on the beach, this is a place for languorous days around the infinity pool, sprawled across cushioned lounge chairs with a Dark and Stormy in one hand and a Rachel Cusk novel in the other, and long, slow dinners overlooking the ocean. Its spot on St. Catherine’s Beach—so named for the centuries-old fort that sits on a headland above it—is actually one of the most historic spots in Bermuda. It’s here where, in 1609, Admiral Sir George Somers and the first colonists landed after their ship,
Sea Venture
, was wrecked on a reef en route to the Jamestown Colony in Virginia; these 150 survivors would go on to permanently settle the territory. (The strip is also known as “Gates’ Bay” after Sir Thomas Gates, a commander traveling on the
Sea Venture
.)
There’s an 18-hole Robert Trent Jones Sr.-designed golf course, too, just across the way from the resort’s main entrance, so you’ll find a cadre of 30- and 40-somethings, decked out in sweat-wicking baseball caps and short-sleeve polos swaggering back from a day of play, along with young families meeting up with other young families, and couples celebrating or just taking a long weekend from the East Coast. (It’s a quick flight, at just under two hours from Newark via United, for example). There were a lot of babymooners in particular, but it made perfect sense: it’s an easy trip, and though it’s an island resort, hardcore drinking and partying isn’t really on the menu. Sure, the cocktails are great and you can sip them to your heart’s content—but at its core, this place is really all about R&R. All you have to do is get yourself here, and they’ll take care of the rest.
The backstory:
Somewhat unusually for the brand, its founders, the titan Astor family, have a history here in Bermuda; John Jacob Astor’s son, Vincent Astor, maintained a 22-acre estate here in St. George’s Ferry Reach. And St. Catherine’s Beach, where the hotel sits, has a long history in hospitality. Once a private beach, a Holiday Inn first opened on the site in 1973, before it was bought by the New York-based Loews Hotels in 1981. Just four years later, it changed hands yet again, reopening as a 630-room Club Med before Hurricane Emily hit in 1987—but the storm wrought severe damage on the property, and the next year, it closed for good. The hotel lay derelict for 20 years until it was demolished in 2008, and it wasn’t until 2018—after a full decade—that the St. Regis broke ground. Construction was completed some three years (and three hurricanes, one pandemic, and two COVID-19 lockdowns) later, and in May, the property was finally able to open its doors.
The resort maintains plenty of the more classic St. Regis traditions, including a nightly Champagne sabering and an afternoon tea ritual; it even offers four kinds of Bloody Marys, a drink originally invented at the hotel’s first location in New York. (The Gates Bay Mary, a version concocted for this specific location, uses Gosling’s Black Seal rum and a tangy sherry pepper sauce.) But I also liked the way many of the spaces managed to give a sense of place without veering too far into the beach theme. I spent lots of time ogling the large-scale, pastel-hued paintings by Venezuelan artist Andrés Michelena in the lobby that somehow delivered just the right amount of island flair, depicting cerulean horizons and homes with Bermuda’s trademark stepped white roofs; and in the St. Regis Bar, Brooklyn-based artist Johanna Burke’s mosaic mural, a mess of swelling, green-blue waves, was inspired by
The Tempest
, Shakespeare’s final play, which is said to be based on the wreck of the
Sea Venture
. (You can even see little hands at the bottom; at the end of
The Tempest
, the character Prospero asks the audience to clap him off of the stage in order to free him.)
St. Catherine’s Beach is also now public—so though about half of it is taken up by the St. Regis’s beach set-up (including cabanas and lounge chairs and their accompanying umbrellas), you can also find locals hanging out here, pitching colorful tents on the sand or kicking around a soccer ball in the waves at dusk. And honestly, with a beach this perfect, who could blame them?
The rooms:
With 120 rooms, 21 of which are suites, the resort feels like just the right size. Neither boutique nor big-box, you’re bound to see more than a few familiar faces around the pool each day. There are also two as-yet-unopened residences that will add another 30 keys: Jobson’s Cove, to be completed by the end of the summer, and Gates Bay, which will open by the end of 2022. In general, rooms felt in keeping with the resort’s upscale but relaxed vibe, with shades of mint green and blue and light stone used throughout; in ours, a Grand Luxe with an oceanfront balcony, you entered through the bathroom, which had a massive, oblong tub squarely in the center. Positioned vertically toward the picture-perfect view beyond, it was buffeted by stacks of crystal-edged stone—a practical design choice, so that if you order room service, the attendant can leave your tray inside without being able to see into the tub. There was also a shower nook, stocked with Le Labo products, to one side of the bathtub, behind a frosted glass door, that had both regular and rainfall showerheads. The pressure was perfect and the temperature remained consistent throughout even a 40-minute shower (guilty!)—and snuggling into a plush Frette bathrobe and slippers rounded it all out nicely. A set of sliding wooden doors led into the bedroom, which was furnished with a king bed, a small couch, and a dresser, plus a television. The decor didn’t quite blow me away—but the bed, with its fluffy white linens, was incredibly comfortable and the air conditioning was nice and strong. Of course, the real delight was the spacious balcony, which looked out over the two pools and onto the sparkling ocean below. Pulling back the curtains each morning to take in the sight of the water, glinting like a six-million carat diamond in the sunlight, was easily what I looked forward to most.
Though each of the rooms and suites have their own private balconies, not every room has that head-on ocean view; so when you’re booking, make sure to distinguish between “oceanfront” and “oceanview” rooms. (The former have that million-dollar view directly over the water, while the latter look out over the golf course, a pond, or in some cases, a parking lot, with the ocean further beyond.)
Food and drink:
The food was, to my mind, one of the most impressive—and impressively consistent—parts about this resort. And it was consistent across venues, too: the food we ate on the beach, packaged in interlocking plastic boxes, was every bit as good as the polished plates we had at dinner at BLT Steak NY. The food itself was light and fresh, exactly what you’d want when you’re sitting under the sun all day—and incorporates lots of local bounty, particularly the abundant fresh fish. Executive chef Julio Ferradas, who comes to the St. Regis Bermuda from the
JW Marriott Lima
, has loaded up the various menus with more traditionally Bermudian offerings like fried fish sandwiches and low-key pool and beach eats like bacon cheeseburgers, fresh chicken wraps, and quinoa salads—but it’s the way he’s managed to incorporate his own Peruvian heritage that impressed me most, from delicate ceviches with hunks of rockfish to huancaina dip and fish tacos with expertly fried local wahoo and zesty chipotle sauce.
The offerings vary slightly between in-room dining, The St. Regis Bar, the pool and beach, and Lina, the all-day restaurant; by night, the latter becomes BLT Steak NY, where the menu is (predictably) heavy on the red meat offerings: New York strip, cowboy, Kansas City, filet mignon, which you can order with attendant sauces like bearnaise, horseradish, red wine, and chimichurri. (The aged porterhouse for two was excellent, though we asked for it rare and it was a bit overcooked.) But don’t sleep on the fish offerings: the crab cake and silky burrata appetizers were two of the tastiest things I ate while I was there. I was equally impressed by the beverage program: there were, of course, classic Bermuda staples like the Rum Swizzle and Dark and Stormy, but I found a few new warm-weather favorites, too, including a pineapple mezcal sour, topped with foamy egg white, and the Smoky Capa, a heady blend of Bacardi, housemade spice syrup, and fresh lime juice. I’m stunned to say it—but in four days, over six meals and plenty of rounds of cocktails, I didn’t eat or drink anything that didn’t delight me (and I’m pretty picky that way).
The spa:
There’s a small spa located on the ground floor of the hotel, right next to the gym; it has its own relaxation area, an enclosed outdoor space between the locker rooms and the treatment rooms, with a plunge pool where you’re meant to chill pre- and post-treatment. There are three types of massage on offer: deep muscle tension release, a personalized massage, and a “relaxing” massage that uses light pressure. (You can also add on “enhancements,” like a collagen face mask and a scalp massage.) During our stay, there was only one spa therapist on the property, which isn’t ideal for a resort that’s primed for serious R&R (and not much else). But my massage with Elix was nice, and I left the treatment feeling pretty blissed out—so the issue should be remedied once the property is able to hire a few more hands.
The neighborhood/area:
The resort is on St. George’s Island, just a few minutes’ drive from the Historic Town of St. George’s, Bermuda’s first capital. (Both the town and St. Catherine’s Beach, where the resort sits, are considered protected UNESCO World Heritage Sites, along with a few other local fortifications and sites). These days, it’s pretty sleepy—much quieter than the current capital of Hamilton—but there’s plenty to see in town, particularly if you’re a history buff. We took a tour with local guide Peter Frith, an enigmatic, eleventh-generation Bermudian who took us to sites like
St. Peter’s Church
and the State House, built in 1620, which once housed Bermuda’s Parliament; Kristin White, the owner of local bookstore
Long Story Short
(and whose son William works part-time at the hotel),
also leads
walking and bicycle tours around town. After strolling around and buying a proper pair of pastel Bermuda shorts from the English Sports Shop on Front Street, you can refuel with a fried fish sandwich from local joint Wahoo’s; served on cinnamon raisin toast, each bite has a sweet tang that feels like a reward. And don’t miss nearby Tobacco Beach, which is just across the road from the resort. Its shallow waters stretch for several hundred yards, making it perfect for snorkeling. It’s also a local spot, with a bar and a DJ. When we went on a Sunday, it was bumping—apparently, that’s the day to go—with the DJ playing reggae hits from the early aughts.
The service:
The resort staff was a mix of savvy locals and hospitality industry veterans plucked from other properties around the world—but they were all people we came to know and looked forward to seeing around during our stay. There was Ben, our “butler,” who knew just the right questions to ask (about coffee; about extra pillows; about making sure our beach cabana was set up in advance) and didn’t miss a beat; Mirian, the sweet bartender whose Gates Bay Bloody Mary was so delicious that it managed to convert my Bloody Mary-hating fiancé; Mildred, our server at the St. Regis Bar, who celebrated her birthday while we were there; De, another bartender, and a passionate guy who took joy in telling us about the cocktail he’d created; Shirley, who freshened up our room each day, so that it barely looked like anyone had just dragged themselves in from the beach; and Teddy, who took care of us down at the beach and brought us water with almost clock-like regularity. And that's the short list.
In general, the service was good, but there were a few snags and inconsistencies that felt like early growing pains: we only received turn-down service on one of the three nights of our stay, for instance; and there seemed to be an understaffing issue, which came into sharp focus on Sunday morning, as a rush of guests tried to grab one last breakfast at Lina before heading to the airport. (The resort is trying to hire 150 more people, predominantly waitstaff, housekeeping, and guest services, so this should improve.) But those were the exceptions—and in general I felt well-attended to, and as though the team was happy to be there.
For families:
Families can spend whole days by the pool and in the ocean. There’s a designated children’s pool (which is the same as the second pool, minus the hot tub) where kids can splash around while parents are tended to by the pool staff, who can deliver a steady stream of cocktails and fish tacos. But beyond that, there isn’t too much to keep little ones occupied, at least not yet. (There’s a Kids’ Club on the ground-floor of the hotel, by the gym and spa, but it’s still closed because of COVID-19; the hotel hopes to open it up in September or October, if conditions improve, and it’ll offer a regular calendar of events.) Currently, there isn’t any gear you can rent for snorkeling, scuba diving, paddle boarding, and the like (though the resort is working on it)—so kids will enjoy playing in the pool and on the beach, but not much else. The kids we saw seemed to be enjoying themselves completely—and so did the parents, for that matter. But for now, if you want to do anything other than play in the sand, you can enlist the front office and the butler team to help book offsite activities.
Eco effort:
The hotel team has participated in beach clean-ups on St. Catherine’s Beach and nearby Tobacco Bay, which are common in Bermuda; the property also uses paper straws at the bar and restaurant, and refillable bottles for bathroom toiletries.
Accessibility:
The beach isn’t currently ADA-accessible, though the two pools are. The resort has seven ADA-accessible rooms.
Anything left to mention?
It was so easy to get here from New York—less than a two-hour plane ride, and a 10-minute drive from the airport through absolutely zero traffic—that I was embarrassed not to have visited before. And that sense of ease extended to the rest of our time there: we were well-taken care of by a team of the loveliest, most obliging folks. It’s not cheap, but the direct access to a beach of this caliber is almost unquantifiable. There were some hiccups, which is to be expected of any new property, but much of that should be fixed by the hiring of more staff and the opening of some of the offerings that are currently on pause, like the Kids’ Club. But all told, I couldn’t believe I got to wake up there each morning.
