Fairmont Hotel Macdonald
Edmonton's original grand hotel, a 1915 river-bluff château that still anchors the city.

Best for:
Special-occasion travelers, groups, and history lovers
Location:
Downtown Edmonton overlooking the North Saskatchewan River
Can’t-miss feature:
The large back patio overlooking the river valley
In a nutshell
: The last of Edmonton's grand railway hotels, the Fairmont is a 1915 Châteauesque landmark on a bluff above the North Saskatchewan River, renovated in 2024 and the city's most convincing argument for staying downtown.
Rooms
There are 198 rooms across eight floors. The palette is neutral and calm—quartz counters, heavy drapery, crystal light fixtures—with the heritage handled through bespoke artwork made from century-old photographs of the hotel. Beds are Fairmont's
signature setup
: a properly supportive king dressed in crisp white linens, with plush robes hanging in the closet for the walk down to the pool. Bathrooms are where the renovation money clearly went—illuminated mirrors, full glass walk-in rain showers in the king and queen rooms (doubles get a tub-shower combo), and Le Labo toiletries.
The catch is the windows: some city-facing rooms make do with a single narrow one, so the room can feel dim even on a clear prairie afternoon (or evening—during the summer, sunset can clock in at 10:30 p.m.). Book river-facing if the rate allows; the valley view is the big selling point of booking a stay here. Fairmont Gold (the property’s “hotel within a hotel” concept) occupies the top two floors, with private seventh-floor check-in and a lounge where the evening canapés are substantial enough to quietly stand in for dinner. I did not get to experience this but I
did
snoop around the seventh floor after an early morning workout, and the lounge area looked quite nice.
If you really want to go all out, the splurgiest bookings in the house are the named suites—Churchill, Edward VIII, Elizabeth II—each honoring a guest who actually stayed here. The Queen Elizabeth suite, renovated for her own 2005 Alberta tour, sprawls across 2,400 square feet on the third floor and is reachable only by private elevator.
Noah Cortez
Noah Cortez
Food and drink
The Harvest Room handles three-meals-a-day duty with river views and a menu built on Alberta beef (the region is known for it), wild game, Ocean Wise seafood, and herbs cut from the hotel's own garden. I ordered the breakfast sandwich with spiced heirloom potatoes on the side— it was pretty tasty, and just what I needed before a five-hour drive to Jasper. At dinner, the move is the AAA Alberta prime rib with Kennebec mash and house-made Yorkshire pudding. The weekend afternoon tea also has a deserved local following.
The Confederation Lounge is where you really want to be. The bar leans hard into house-made everything: cocktails built with the hotel's backyard honey, homemade bitters, and a private-label bourbon that anchors their take on an old fashioned (with lapsang tea syrup standing in for the usual sugar). The lounge also claims the rarest Dalmore collection in Canada, if your evening calls for a $300 pour of Scotch smoked tableside with vanilla and chocolate tobacco. If you’d rather eat in your robe (can’t blame ya), in-room dining runs around the clock.
Noah Cortez
Amenities and experiences
The fitness center is
huge
—it’s two full floors with a ton of equipment and machines, including an international squash court, saunas, and a steam room, which wouldn't be what you expect tucked inside a 1915 château—and the 40-foot indoor saltwater pool is low-lit and quiet, more akin to a spa, with a hot tub alongside.
The hotel keeps a resident Canine Ambassador on staff, and leans into its namesake's Scottish roots with a house tartan (staff occasionally turn up in kilts, allegedly). I recommend spending a few minutes with the black-and-white photos on the mezzanine level: in the earliest skyline shots, The Mac is essentially the only building on the riverbank.
What to do nearby
The hotel sits where downtown meets the river valley—North America's largest stretch of urban parkland—and a glass-sided funicular drops you straight onto the trails. Don't miss a stroll along the hotel's own patio overlooking the river before you go anywhere else. From there, the Art Gallery of Alberta and Royal Alberta Museum are a 10-minute walk, Rogers Place (Oilers games, arena shows) about 15, and the Muttart Conservatory's glass pyramids sit down in the valley itself. For food, Olia serves up some killer Italian and is about 10 minutes away by car. (Ubers are plentiful here if you aren’t renting one.)
Noah Cortez
The backstory
The Grand Trunk Pacific Railway opened the hotel in July 1915, with Montreal firm Ross and MacFarlane designing it in full Châteauesque style—part of the same coast-to-coast grand-hotel project as the Château Frontenac and Banff Springs.
It's named for Sir John A. Macdonald, Canada's first prime minister, and it's been collecting royal history ever since: When King George VI and the Queen Mother stayed during their 1939 tour, the crowds outside reportedly caused Edmonton's first traffic jam. For over a century it anchored the city's skyline—Edmonton's downtown quite literally grew up around it—and the 2024 renovation earned it a Michelin Key the following year.
Insider tip
For all its stone and scale, sound carries here—hallway traffic noise (read: screaming kids)
can
find its way into your room, depending how booked up the hotel is. Light sleepers should bring ear plugs.
Final considerations:
Family friendly:
Yes
Accessibility:
Accessible rooms are available; request a roll-in shower specifically and confirm layout in advance
Wellness:
Yes, the two-floor health club has a sauna and steam room, and there are three treatment rooms if you want book a massage
