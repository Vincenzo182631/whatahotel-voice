Salterra, a Luxury Collection Resort & Spa
South Caicos, with a local population of a thousand, feels a world away from the buzz of Providenciales.

Reviewed by
Hannah Singleton
Why book?
Resorts love to use their locale as a pretty backdrop, but guests at Salterra actually leave with a better understanding of South Caicos, a small fishing island off the main Turks and Caicos strip. The island’s salt-harvesting past is the throughline for everything—from the choice of accent colors in the rooms to the stories you hear over a five-course dinner. As one of only two resorts on the island, the main draw is how uncrowded and close to nature it feels. On its two-mile stretch of beach, you won’t be scrambling for a front-row lounge chair, and the adventure team is basically on standby to get you into (or onto) that electric-blue water that the Caribbean is known for.
Set the scene
The drive from the South Caicos airport to Salterra takes less than ten minutes, but it’s enough time to wonder where exactly you’ve landed. You’ll pass salt ponds, a few low houses, a stray donkey or two, and—if you’re lucky—dots of pink flamingos clustered on the horizon. The island feels mostly empty, because it is. Then the resort doors swing open and you’re in paradise. Staff welcomes you with hibiscus tea in a breezy, light-soaked lobby with coral stone walls and thatched straw pendant lamps. Around the property, designers chose the decor to mimic the landscape: the low-slung chairs in the lobby are reminiscent of ancient Taino ceremonial chairs (
duhos
) found in caves nearby, and the colour palette is pulled directly from a drone photo of the salt flats at sunset.
Salterra sits on a nearly private stretch of beach on the island’s southern edge (although all beaches are public in Turks and Caicos, there’s no public entry point.) There are multiple restaurants, a spa, a gym, an adults-only pool, and an adventure team eager to take you snorkeling or boating. The resort feels quiet, but not lifeless, and the crowd that
is
there runs the gamut: honeymooners looking for a slower pace, retired couples who want to chat up strangers by the bar, and the occasional girls’ trip, realising they may not find any nightlife around here. During the festive season (late November to early January), maybe you’ll see kids buzzing around, but in early December, it felt like an adults-only resort.
The backstory
On a small island with limited infrastructure, building a luxury resort takes a lot of ambition, of course, but also restraint and respect for the people who already live there. After getting to know Salterra founder Michael Tibbets, he strikes me as perfectly positioned to handle that tension with care. Tibbetts grew up around his family’s small scuba dive lodges in the Cayman Islands—Little Cayman Beach Resort and Cayman Brac Beach Resort—where he learned the unglamorous yet necessary logistics of remote hospitality, like how to desalinate water or source as much local food as possible.
When a friend tipped him off to the South Caicos property, the resort was running at a much smaller scale. Tibbetts spent a year building a relationship with the aging owner who had no succession plan, then teamed up with Marriott Luxury Collection, which came on as the umbrella brand. He studied the history of the island and met with locals before renovating the main building and adding new structures for more suites. Salterra opened in March 2025 and now spans 85 acres, with future plans to add low-density residential villas along the coastline.
The rooms
You don’t have to splurge on a top-tier suite to get an ocean view here. The property is arranged in a crescent shape so that all 46 rooms and 54 suites (100 in total) face the beach. Inside, the rooms are clean, calming, and intentionally sparse; pale wood accents, soft pink and peachy tones, and white stone floors. The coastal minimalism still has just enough character to feel warm and cozy—and looks much more glamorous in real life than it does in photos. Everything still feels fresh from the grand opening.
I stayed in a one-bedroom suite, which felt more like a small apartment. The bedroom has a king bed, a daybed, an armoire, a desk, and side tables; the separate living room has a sectional sofa (with a pull-out couch), a full kitchenette, a large counter, and a private patio. First-floor units are especially good for families: they open onto a private, landscaped lanai with a hammock, so kids can roam a bit without you having to worry about a balcony ledge.
Bathrooms are more than generously sized, with a delightful double rainfall shower, plus large soaking tubs in some units, Byredo products, and nice lighting. The room came stocked with the full lineup of Salterra-branded extras: shave kit, hairbrush, comb, shower cap, dental kit, and more.
There are also more compact units without a living room, as well as two-bedroom suites. Larger families or groups should look into one of the two presidential suites, which include two standard bedrooms and a kids’ bunk room (complete with stuffed flamingos, donkeys, and sting rays). The space is palatial with vaulted ceilings, a full kitchen with an island, a dining table, and even a telescope to stargaze at night. Also nice: the minibar comes stocked with complimentary snacks and drinks.
Food and drink
You’ll have the choice of six on-site dining options, including everything from a food truck to fine dining. But I found myself particularly drawn to Cobo Bar and Grill—while the other restaurants are a few meters inland, this one sits closest to the lapping waves, so you feel the ocean breeze as you sip. Every day, I would stroll down from my suite to Cobo for lunch or pre-dinner drinks. The shrimp aguachile and bacon-wrapped jalapeño were good enough to order the next day again, and the conch—a local delicacy—is worth trying at least once.
For breakfast, Regatta (named after the annual island sailing event each May) is the place for a proper sit-down meal. But I usually preferred something lighter: a coffee, a pastry, or a smoothie from Flamingo Café. At dinner, Regatta shifts to a British-Caribbean menu that changes seasonally. You can expect fish-forward staples, like saltfish, lobster, or snapper. For late-night drinks, Sisal has a knowledgeable mixology team and a twist on a tropical cocktail menu, with highlights like a Smokey Mai Tai and Velour Night.
The main draw, though, is Brine, which has an elegant, earthy feel: rust-coloured velvet chairs, terracotta-toned pillows, and sculptural rattan pendant lights that hang from the ceiling like upside-down flower petals. The five-course (soon to be seven) tasting menu is an experiential look into the salt-mining past: each dish is paired with a different flavoured salt. For the fourth course, I sprinkled bone marrow and rosemary salt onto the succulent beef tenderloin to brighten up the rich dish.
The spa
The spa setting is one of the nicest on property: It’s tucked into a quiet courtyard with whitewashed gables, a private plunge pool, and scalloped striped umbrellas, and I felt more like I was at a friend’s Bermudan guesthouse than a hotel spa. It got so popular that Salterra now requires a day pass for guests who aren’t booking a treatment. A day pass grants access to the pool, sauna, steam room, hot tub, and newly opened hammam. Signature treatments include a cleansing salt ritual that—you guessed it—nods to the island’s salt-harvesting history. The spa uses products from Wildflower, a local Turkish brand, and Voya, an organic skincare brand with seaweed-based products.
The area
South Caicos, with a local population of 1,000, is quiet, without a downtown or a cruise port. There’s only one other major resort on the island. You can access everything you might want to visit, like the handful of local restaurants and bars, by grabbing one of the free beach cruiser bikes in the hotel entrance and pedaling a few minutes into Cockburn Harbor. (If you ask the Salterra staff the best local spot in town, they’ll recommend you head over to Triple J’s for ribs and jerk chicken.)
Most people don’t come here looking for thriving nightlife or a long list of dining options, though. Nature and marine life are really what you’re here for. I booked a boat tour to a nearby cay, where iguanas were as ubiquitous as seashells. My guide, Cameron, gave me water shoes, which he recommended I wear because of my red toenail polish (the iguanas get curious and may nip). We hiked to an overlook that seemed to split the water in two: pale turquoise shallows on one side, and deep blue open ocean on the other. On the way back, we stopped at a shallow patch of sea and strapped on snorkeling masks to look at the giant, purple starfish resting on the white sands.
The service
The place is small enough that the staff knows your name, but feels familial and caring, not overbearing. Staff checks you in while sitting in the gorgeous lobby—not at a front desk—and each room has a discreet light outside the door to signal when you’d like housekeeping service. Suites have butlers, who you can message on WhatsApp for anything you need.
By the second morning, I’d barely made it through the lobby before my butler, Tony, called out “Good morning, madam!” and asked if I had any plans for the day. I told him I was heading to the island’s coral lab to learn about coral restoration. He nodded like he already knew I’d love it, then reminded me to text him if I needed anything—he didn’t want me wandering around looking for help. “Make sure you eat a solid breakfast,” he added. “You’ve got a big day ahead.”
And the rest of the staff is the same. Lesie, the bartender at Cobo, greeted me each day with a big smile and an anecdote about adjusting to island life after working at more posh properties. On my last day, two members of the adventure team ran up to the front desk to say goodbye. Earlier, I had told them I was nervous to get back in the water after a particularly bad jellyfish sting from a previous trip. They laughed as they handed me a small parting gift: Sting Master ointment. I hope I never need it, but I know who I’ll message if I do.
For families
I didn’t see any kids during my visit, but that doesn’t mean the property isn’t kid-friendly. The island is relatively quiet and remote and the property is small, so there may not be tons for kids to explore. There’s also a complimentary kids’ club (no parents allowed) with supervised, playful, and educational activities for kids aged four to 12.
Eco effort
As an environmental studies master’s grad, it takes a lot to impress me on sustainability. But Salterra really puts its money where its mouth is (quite literally): it’s working not just to lower the resort’s footprint, but to improve environmental infrastructure across South Caicos.
Of course, they’ve covered the basics—reusable water bottles for all guests to cut down on single-use plastics, plus refill stations and bulk bathroom amenities. But the more ambitious work goes on behind the scenes. The team is working with the local utility company to expand the solar battery capacity so the resort can actually use more of the power it generates. They’re also in the middle of a waste management project that tackles an island-wide problem of building the supply-chain logistics to export recyclables for proper processing (right now, everything gets burned at the local dump) and are helping bring composting to the island.
The hotel also funds environmental non-profits like the Royal Society for the Protection of Birds and often houses visiting scientists who come to the island for research. They also co-founded the South Caicos Coral Reef Consortium, and guests can join a full coral restoration expedition. Starting at the School for Field Studies’ coral lab, you learn the techniques researchers use to regrow and replant vulnerable reef species, then snorkel or dive at local sites to see the result of the hard work.
Accessibility
Salterra is part of Marriott’s Luxury Collection, which follows ADA accessibility standards. You can take a lift to the upper-level rooms and use the ramps throughout the property. While the walkways are made of coral “stones”, they’re smoothly paved and easy to navigate with a wheelchair or stroller.
Anything left to mention?
Don’t skip a nighttime walk on the beach, especially if there’s a full moon. The white sand seems to glow in the dark, and on clear nights, you can spot constellations from one of two floating platforms anchored just offshore. They’re a short, knee-deep walk in the ocean, but feel like your own private raft in the middle of the sea.
