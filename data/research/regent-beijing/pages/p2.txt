Regent Beijing
One of the most dependable luxury stays in central Beijing.

Reviewed by
Laura Ratliff
Why book?
This is a polished, old-school luxury hotel in the heart of Beijing that still gets the basics right: rooms are oversized (including marble bathrooms with soaking tubs) and it’s set in a genuinely useful location near Wangfujing and the Forbidden City.
Set the scene
Regent Beijing is located on Jinbao Street in Dongcheng, right between old imperial Beijing and the modern commercial city. You’re a short walk from Wangfujing’s shopping district, close to the Forbidden City and Tiananmen Square, and directly above Dengshikou subway station, which makes getting around Beijing easy—especially for a city of this size.
The hotel itself feels very much like a classic international luxury property from the late 2000s, but it’s not a knock. The atrium lobby, sparkling stone floors, and formal staff create an atmosphere hard to find in newer, aesthetic-driven lifestyle hotels.
The crowd skews heavily toward business travelers, international tour groups, airline crews, and affluent multigenerational families. It’s not an especially scene-y hotel, but that’s also part of the appeal.
The backstory
Opened in 2007 as part of the Regent Hotels & Resorts portfolio, Regent Beijing arrived as the city was rapidly expanding its luxury hospitality scene ahead of the Olympics.
But rather than reinvent Chinese luxury hospitality, the hotel embraced a more timeless international model: large room sizes, polished service, extensive wellness facilities, and multiple destination restaurants. Now, nearly two decades later, the formula still works surprisingly well here.
The property has 496 rooms and suites and remains one of the more established luxury addresses in central Beijing. While newer luxury hotels in the city may feel more design-forward, Regent’s consistency, location, and value proposition still keep it among the best the city has to offer, especially at its price-point.
The rooms
The rooms are large by almost any city standard, starting at nearly 500 square feet for the entry-level Classic Room. Even the most basic categories include a proper work setup, seating area, and oversized marble bathrooms.
Decor falls firmly into the “contemporary luxury circa 2007” category: walnut wood furniture, plush carpets, cream and beige tones, lacquer accents, and the occasional Chinese-inspired art piece. It’s dated, but not offensively so—especially given that the rooms themselves are extremely functional. Floor-to-ceiling windows let in lots of natural light, the blackout curtains work well, and the beds are both supportive and plush.
Even standard rooms come with separate soaking tubs and rain showers, plus enough counter space to fully unpack toiletries without balancing things precariously beside the sink.
If the budget allows, it’s worth upgrading to a high-floor room or to the Regent Club category. The higher floors offer better skyline views and occasional glimpses toward the Forbidden City. The Regent Club lounge on the 17th floor is one of the hotel’s strongest perks, with a surprisingly peaceful atmosphere, solid breakfast service, and all-day refreshments.
Food and drink
Regent Beijing quietly has one of the better dining set-ups of Beijing’s hotels. Lei Garden is the biggest draw. The Hong Kong-based Cantonese restaurant group is known for helping popularize XO sauce, and the Beijing outpost serves polished dim sum, roasted meats, and other Cantonese classics in a glamorous dining room.
Daccapo serves upscale Italian fare, including handmade pastas, risotto, and seafood, in a warm, relatively intimate dining room. Meanwhile, Morton’s of Chicago feels like a familiar international luxury-hotel power dinner spot, complete with private dining suites and oversized steaks.
For something more casual, Jinbao 99 functions as the all-day dining restaurant, with open kitchens and a broad mix of Asian and international dishes. The Lobby Lounge is also worth mentioning for afternoon tea or late-evening cocktails accompanied by live music.
The neighborhood/area
Dongcheng is one of the most practical areas in Beijing for first-time visitors. You’re close to many of the city’s biggest landmarks without feeling trapped in a touristy neighborhood.
Wangfujing’s shopping streets are nearby for luxury retail and snack stalls, while the hutongs and narrower historic alleys around the Forbidden City are easy to explore from here. Tiananmen Square is less than a mile away, and the Forbidden City is roughly 1.5 miles from the hotel.
The subway access is also a major advantage. Dengshikou Station is essentially at the hotel’s doorstep—which is much easier than relying exclusively on Beijing traffic.
The service
Service lands somewhere between polished business-hotel professionalism and genuine warmth. Staff members are efficient without feeling robotic, and the hotel handles early arrivals, concierge requests, and lounge service especially well. The concierge team can help navigate Beijing’s logistics-heavy sightseeing schedule, from transportation planning to restaurant reservations.
Accessibility
The hotel includes elevators, wheelchair access to common areas, and accessible public facilities. Its direct proximity to subway access also improves mobility around the city for many travelers.
Anything left to mention?
Ask specifically for a high-floor room facing toward central Beijing. The views are dramatically better, and the extra natural light makes the rooms feel much fresher.
