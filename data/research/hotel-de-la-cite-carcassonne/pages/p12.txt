Reviewed by
Sophie Friedman
Why book?
In the heart of the medieval citadel Cité de Carcassonne, a midsize boutique hotel with a design style that mixes Middle Ages with Collegiate Gothic. A heated pool—a godsend during Carcassonne’s often sweltering summer—and a beautiful dining room sweeten the deal.
Set the scene
If you’ve ever toured a medieval-era university campus in the UK, the decor at Hôtel de la Cité Carcassonne will feel familiar, down to the ivy-draped exterior. The restaurant and bar lean into the dark academia/eating club look—think bookshelves, cognac-colored leather club chairs, and a fireplace. And you know, it works. After all, this is a hotel built into a citadel from the Middle Ages, though with a pool, spa, and pain au chocolats on hand, guests here are living eons better than the Gallo-Romans used to.
Today guests come not from Rome as the Cité’s founders did, but from across France, northern Spain (the border is just two hours away), England (there are direct Ryanair flights to Carcassonne’s small airport), as well as the US.
The hotel has a formal feel, with lots of wood, leather, and heavy chandeliers, but no one is excessively dressed up. Though we saw one come to dinner in matching tracksuits, most other guests were attired in solid business casual. This doesn’t mean no shorts—far from it. Carcassonne is very hot in summer (though the hotel is air-conditioned). Still, no one will be walking from the pool to their room in a robe; be sure to pack a cover-up.
The backstory
Though the (neo) Gothic building looks quasi-medieval to fit in with its surroundings, most of it dates to 1909, when the hotel had just six rooms. In 1928, the original owners took over a hotel that was mere feet across one of the narrow cobblestone streets that make up the citadel and added 48, keeping the neo-Gothic style, including pretty leaded windows.
Fast forward 70-ish years, and the hotel was sold to Orient Express, who managed it until 2011 when the Pujol family, who own several hotels in the area, snapped it up. Today, the Pujol family owns the hotel, and it’s part of MGallery, one of French hospitality group Accor's eight luxury brands (incidentally, these include Orient Express).
What all of this means is that you can earn (and burn) Accor points while staying here, but it’s still a family-owned property with a boutique feel.
The rooms
If the public spaces lean dark academia, the rooms are a little homier, a bit reminiscent of your grandparents’ house; contemporary design this is not, and it’s not trying to be. They’re all slightly different sizes, but each has thick curtains over leaded windows, headboards in mahogany or light gray fabric, Regency-style chairs, and candelabra scones. The marble that makes up the bathrooms comes from nearby quarries; some have marble from a nearby quarry, and others have the same reddish marble used at Le Petit Trianon at Versailles.
Classic Rooms look out onto the streets of the Citadel; we recommend plumping for a Superior room for views of the castle’s massive ramparts and the hotel’s garden. Of the now 46 rooms and 13 suites, 12 have private terraces facing the same way.
Rooms are in good condition but could use some updating; on our visit, the mattress sagged a bit, a bedside lamp didn’t work, several light switches didn't seem to be connected, and the bathroom light switch is all the way next to the mirror.
Food and drink
With its heavy wooden chandeliers and candelabra sconces, velvet-striped carved wooden chairs, and a border of family crests, La Barbican looks very much like the eating club at an esteemed, centuries-old university. Were the hotel not indeed within a medieval city, this would be too much; as it is, it’s a lovely ambiance, especially during the cooler months. The dining room’s pointed arch windows look out onto the ancient ramparts and enormous towers; when the weather is nice, meal service is outdoors on the patio directly in front of the castle, and it’s particularly pretty at night
La Barbican serves fine French fare and, as is traditional in French cuisine, uses local and seasonal ingredients. Fortunately, the growing season is long in the southwest of France, an area of the country
covered
in vineyards, so there’s no shortage of excellent regional wine, olive oil, dairy, etc.
Both lunch and dinner here are multicourse set menus that can change based on what’s in season; winter might feature scallops with a “risotto” made with chestnuts, black chanterelles, and Comté cheese. If you prefer to order à la carte, or if you’re someone who prefers strong flavors, you’d do better to eat in town. Since breakfast is also served at La Barbican, you’ll still have ample time to enjoy the neo-Gothic ambiance.
If you’ve got kids in tow or are after a casual bite, the hotel’s Boulangerie de la Cité has good quiches and sandwiches, as well as elaborate desserts. Everything can be enjoyed in the garden.
The neighborhood/area
The hotel is in the core of Carcassonne’s medieval citadel, a maze of winding, narrow streets lined with restaurants, stores selling leather goods, and souvenir shops (if you’re traveling with kids, you’re not getting out of here without at least two fake swords). Tourists come to Carcassonne specifically for the UNESCO-listed fortified city, whose enormous stone castle and ramparts, some of which date to the Roman era, can be explored. It’s pretty crowded here, but the hotel is not on a narrow street but in a relatively quiet plaza, far from empty but with a smaller flow of sword-toting visitors.
Carcassonne train station and historic center of town are just across the Aude River from the hotel. In nice weather, people picnic on the grassy banks of the Aude and sit outdoors in Place Carnot, a restaurant-filled plaza with a fountain and market on Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday mornings. Bus number three connects the train station to the Cite, and the hotel can collect you from the bus stop.
The service
With 59 rooms, this hotel hits the sweet spot on size: you can slip in unnoticed if you don’t feel like chatting, but if you need something the staff are ready to help. Most of the staff are politely, if coolly, efficient, but there were a couple of more gregarious waitstaff at the restaurant who were happily chatting with diners, explaining the provenance of various ingredients.
For families
Come during the warmer months if you’re traveling with kids to take advantage of the pool (open May through October, weather permitting.
The hotel has five family rooms, each of which can accommodate up to four people across two single beds and one king. One of the family rooms is a duplex, with two twin beds on the mezzanine. Families can also do well booking the four connecting rooms and requesting single beds where needed.
France is not a country in which kids run amok shouting, so although the garden is a nice place for the junior set to play, bear in mind that the adult guests are trying to enjoy a relaxing stay. Save for the small staff cars, the entire interior of the citadel is pedestrianized, and the hotel itself sits on a wide plaza, so parents can breathe easy if older kids step out on their own.
Accessibility
Carcassonne itself is not terribly wheelchair friendly due to the steep, narrow, cobblestone streets. Because the citadel is a protected UNESCO World Heritage Site, only small staff cars are allowed between 8:30 a.m. and 9:30 p.m. To that end, guests arriving by taxi (or with their own cars) are met at the hotel’s parking lot by a staffer with a tiny manual car who will then zoom through the medieval city gates to deposit you at the hotel’s front door.
There are two fully wheelchair-accessible rooms (104 and 106). Part of the hotel’s charm is its slightly maze-like layout. Some rooms are reached by going up a handsome wooden staircase from the lobby and then taking it down to a garden-level floor. If you have limited mobility or are traveling with a stroller, for example, alert the hotel so they can allocate you a room close to the elevator.
Anything left to mention?
Where else are you going to wake up to a medieval castle and have breakfast in a dining room to match? Though the room decor at this hotel is a bit dated and some of the public spaces were rather cold during our winter visit (the rooms were perfectly toasty), the neo-Gothic ambiance and access to the Cité make it worth a night’s stay or two.
