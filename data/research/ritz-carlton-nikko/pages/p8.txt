The Ritz-Carlton, Nikko
A luxury stay amid some of Japan’s most beautiful temples and rural scenery

Why book?
To join the mountain gods for some peak Japanese luxury in one of the most scenic and serene corners of the country.
Set the scene
Mountain deities are said to live in the forested stratovolcanoes that surround the maple-speckled shores of Lake Chuzenji. It is undoubtedly a home for the gods, who have existed alongside eighth century Buddhist-Shinto monks, three centuries of the Tokugawa Shoganate, and early 20
th
century British, Italian and American diplomats who would retreat to embassy residences over the sweltering summer months. Amid the timeless vista lies the Ritz-Carlton Nikko, a hotel for all seasons. Catch the cherry blossom in spring, stand-up paddle board across the lake in summer, hike through coppery forests in autumn, and, in winter, partake in 'shibuki-gouri', the act of viewing weird and wonderful ice formations.
The backstory
The Ritz-Carlton Nikko is built on the site of the historic Lakeside Hotel, which originally opened in 1898 as a summer retreat aimed at foreign visitors, such as the Victorian explorer Isabella Bird, who chronicled her time in Nikko in her travel journal
Unbeaten Tracks in Japan
. The region was also a hit with wealthy Scottish merchant Thomas Glover, who remarked on Nikko’s resemblance to his homeland. The Scotsman promptly arranged for some brook trout to be imported and released them into the waterways, making Lake Chuzhenji the first place in Japan to have European-style fly-fishing. Only a sliver of the original hotel remains today, in the form of wooden shelves found in the lobby, although you can still find plump trout reeling in the lake.
The rooms
The hotel has 94 rooms including 10 suites, all a soothing sight to behold. Remove your shoes at the entrance and step into a calming cedarwood sanctuary, with subtly textured walls, silently sliding doors, starry Kanuma-kumiko fretwork, puffy white (western-style) beds and cosy balconies where you can wrap yourself in a cashmere blanket, sip kryptonite-green sencha and watch mallards and mute swans flit around the lake. Bathrooms are clad in local Oya stone, a lava and ash rock that’s been used as the foundations for homes for centuries, and have square-shaped onsen so deep you can sink in up to your neck.
Food and drink
Tochigi Prefecture has an ambrosia of farms, orchards, vineyards and sake breweries, which provide the restaurants with much of their produce. Western and Japanese breakfast sets are served in glossy bento boxes at all-day dining restaurant, The Japanese Restaurant, which is set on the first floor overlooking the tranquil gardens. Later, you can feast on sushi, teppanyaki or multi-course kaiseki menus served on mismatched Mashiko ceramics made by home-grown designers. All of the produce is hyper-local and hyper-seasonal. Dishes might include corn and shrimp tempura with bitter melon and onsen salt; just-plucked Tochigi tomatoes with pickled seaweed and fresh ginger; or miso-marinated wagyu. Save space for the soy sauce ice-cream, which has a delightful salted-caramel-like kick.
The Lakehouse is the western restaurant, and it is also fully committed to using local produce. By day, you can stumble in from sightseeing to robust cups of coffee, Nikko cheese sandwiches and French pastries. In the evening, the candlelit mezzanine level opens for dinner; blue lobster and Tochigi pear; seasonal fish and Maitake mushroom with yuzu citrus chili paste; Aramaki Farm apple crumble with cinnamon gelato. End the night at the atmospheric lobby bar drinking cocktails inspired by the ancient love poems of Onono Komachi or, if it’s more to your taste, work your way through a stirring collection of Japanese whiskies.
The spa
Set at ground level the spa and onsen are approached along a covered wooden walkway that meanders through dainty moss gardens dotted with tiny Shinto shrines. Inside, corridors of limestone stone lead to five treatment rooms and separate male and female onsen with indoor and outdoor pools. I suggest a late-night visit, bathing outside steam rising into the cedar-scented air under the jet-black silhouette of Mount Nantai backed by glittering skies and the occasional shooting star.
The neighborhood/area
A set of four special edition Tobu Railway trains make the two-hour journey north from Tokyo’s Tobu Asakusa Station to the historic town of Nikko; try to catch the futuristic Spacia X train, which has hexagonal windows, groovy mulberry and mint green velvet interiors, private suites and its own craft beer. From Nikko station, it’s another 40-minute drive along winding mountain roads through forests of Japanese beech, oak, fir, thuja, and ginko to reach the hotel, which sits in the shadow of Mount Nantai on the banks of Chuzenji Lake. A 10-minute walk from the hotel takes you to Kegon waterfall, considered to be one of Japan’s most beautiful
waterfalls
. In winter, its thundering springs freeze entirely into glittering white fingers like a scene straight out of Narnia.
The service
From the welcoming doormen to the switched-on concierge to the knowledgeable waitstaff and cheerful guides, service is consistently warm, personable and professional. The concierge can arrange an array of genuinely exceptional experiences, including encyclopedic-level tours of the Unesco celebrated temples, shrines and torri gates of Toshugo and a fire blessing ceremony with a Buddhist-Shinto monk inside the 8
th
century Chuzen-ji temple.
For families
Families are welcome, although there’s no kids’ club and the hotel’s hushed atmosphere is better suited to older well-behaved children. Fun family activities include temple walks, cycling tours, visits to Kegon waterfall, stargazing, stand-up paddle boarding and kayaking.
Eco effort
To lower the hotel’s carbon footprint, the build enforced the rigorous use of local materials; Nikko stone, Oya stone, cedarwood and and copper mined from nearby 400-year old Ashio mine. Additionally, hot spring water is sustainably drawn from Yumoto Onsen and most of the produce comes from farms found in Tochigi Prefecture.
Accessibility
The hotel has one ground floor room that has been fully adapted for wheelchair users. Both restaurants and the bar are also accessible, as is the spa, although access to the onsen would require assistance.
Anything left to mention?
While Nikko enjoys a vibrant cherry blossom season its rippling mountain scenery is even more impressive in autumn, when the forests flame from canary yellow to amber and vermillion.
